Three-term labels

Every consonant is named by voicing, place and manner, always in that order: /ʒ/ is a voiced postalveolar fricative.

Fortis and lenis. British textbooks (Roach, Cruttenden) often say fortis/lenis instead of voiceless/voiced, because "voiced" /b d ɡ z/ are often partly devoiced at the start and end of an utterance. What really separates /p/ from /b/ in English is aspiration, muscular force and the length of the vowel before them. Course lists group these differently: some count tap/flap and trill as separate manners, some split approximants into lateral and central, some add labial-velar as its own place. Use the grouping your lecturer uses.

Traps for German speakers

  • Final devoicing. German turns final /b d ɡ v z/ voiceless (Rad = [ʁaːt]). English keeps the contrast: bad /bæd/ vs bat /bæt/, dogs /dɒɡz/ ends in /z/. The vowel before a lenis consonant is longer, so lengthen it.
  • /w/ vs /v/. German has no /w/; German w spells /v/. vest /vest/ vs west /west/, vine vs wine: /w/ is rounded lips with no teeth contact.
  • /θ ð/. Not German sounds. Tongue tip between or against the teeth, not /s z/ or /t d/: think ≠ sink, this ≠ diss.
  • /r/. German /r/ is usually uvular [ʁ]; English /r/ is a postalveolar approximant [ɹ] with no friction.
  • /æ/ vs /e/. German has no /æ/, so bad and bed merge. /æ/ is lower, with the jaw open.
  • /dʒ/ and /ʒ/. Rare in German. judge /dʒʌdʒ/, measure /ˈmeʒə/.
  • Glottal onsets. German puts a glottal stop before word-initial vowels ([ʔ]Apfel). English links smoothly: an apple [ə ˈnæpl̩].

Wells's lexical sets

Each keyword stands for every word that has that vowel. They are the standard way to compare accents: say "the BATH vowel" instead of "the a in bath".

Symbol conventions. This page follows Wells and Roach. RP writes DRESS as /e/ and GA as /ɛ/ (same idea, different tradition). GA transcriptions usually drop the length mark ː. Modern RP (often called SSBE) has moved on in places: TRAP is close to [a], SQUARE is often a long monophthong [ɛː], CURE often merges with NORTH/FORCE (sure /ʃɔː/), GOOSE is fronted toward [ʉː].

Describing a vowel

Vowels get a three-term label too: height (close, close-mid, open-mid, open; plus near-close and near-open), backness (front, central, back) and lip rounding (rounded, unrounded). /uː/ is a close back rounded vowel; /æ/ is near-open front unrounded. Add length and tense/lax where your course asks for it: long vowels /iː ɑː ɔː uː ɜː/ are tense, short /ɪ e æ ʌ ɒ ʊ ə/ are lax.

Diphthongs glide from one quality to another. RP has 8: closing ones that end high, fronting to /ɪ/ (eɪ aɪ ɔɪ) or backing to /ʊ/ (əʊ aʊ), and centring ones that end on schwa (ɪə eə ʊə). GA has no centring diphthongs: those words keep their /r/ instead (near RP /nɪə/, GA /nɪr/).

Phonemes and allophones

A phoneme is a sound that changes meaning (/p/ vs /b/: pin, bin). Its allophones are the different ways it is actually pronounced depending on the context, which never change meaning: the /p/ in pin is aspirated [pʰ], the /p/ in spin isn't. Phonemic transcription goes in slashes, /pɪn/; phonetic (narrow) transcription goes in square brackets and shows the allophones, [pʰɪn]. Test for a phoneme with a minimal pair: two words that differ in one sound only (ship/sheep proves /ɪ/ and /iː/ are separate phonemes).
