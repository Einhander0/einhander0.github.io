Word classes

Framework: descriptive (Quirk), H&P noted

A word class (part of speech, lexical category) is a set of words that behave alike grammatically. You assign a word to a class by its form and its distribution in a particular sentence, not by its meaning, and the same spelling can belong to several classes.

GroupClassesNotes
Opennoun, lexical (full) verb, adjective, adverbNew members are coined all the time (to google, a podcast, cringe as an adjective). These are also the lexical (content) classes: they carry descriptive meaning.
Closeddeterminer, pronoun, preposition, conjunction (coordinator, subordinator), auxiliary verb (primary be, have, do; modals can, will, must...)Small, fixed sets. Mostly functional words: they signal grammatical information such as definiteness, tense or clause type.
Lessernumerals, interjectionsQuirk lists them apart from the main classes.

Open/closed vs lexical/functional. The two splits mostly coincide, but they are different questions: open/closed is about membership, lexical/functional about meaning. Prepositions are the awkward case: a closed class, yet they head full phrases and many have clear spatial meaning (under, behind). Textbooks place P differently, so follow your lecturer.

Criteria for word classes

CriterionWhat you checkExamples
Morphological: inflectionWhich grammatical endings the word takesnouns: plural -s, genitive 's; verbs: -s, -ed, -ing; gradable adjectives: -er, -est (happier)
Morphological: derivationTypical derivational suffixes-ness, -ity, -tion (N); -ize, -ify (V); -ous, -ful, -able (Adj); -ly (Adv). Unreliable alone: friendly, lovely are adjectives.
Distributional (syntactic)Which slots the word fits and what it combines withN: the ___ is here; V: they will ___ it; Adj: a very ___ house, it seems ___; Adv: she did it ___
Semantic (notional)What the word means"A noun names a person, place or thing; a verb is an action." Useful as a rough hint, unusable as a definition (see below).

Why semantic criteria fail. Destruction, arrival, explosion are nouns that denote events (actions). Red and redness denote the same property but are an adjective and a noun. Be, seem, own, resemble are verbs that denote no action. And speakers classify words they have never seen: in the slithy toves did gyre (Lewis Carroll) everyone knows toves is a plural noun and slithy an adjective, purely from form and position. So the class comes first, and the meaning only tends to follow.

Phrases

Framework: neutral (Quirk and Carnie agree here)

Every phrase is built around a head. The head decides the category of the whole phrase and what else may appear in it; it is the one part you cannot leave out. The other parts are dependents: determiners, modifiers and complements.

PhraseHeadExample (head in bold)Typical make-up
NPnoun or pronoun[the old house on the hill]determiner + premodifiers + head + postmodifiers (PP, relative clause, non-finite clause)
VPverb[sold their house last year]verb + complements (objects, predicatives, clauses) + adjuncts
AP (AdjP)adjective[very proud of her son]degree modifier + head + complement (PP, clause)
AdvPadverb[remarkably clearly]degree modifier + head
PPpreposition[right after the concert](modifier) + head + complement, usually an NP

Inside the NP (Quirk): all (predeterminer) the (central determiner) lovely old (premodifiers) houses (head) in the village that we saw (postmodifiers). Nouns can premodify too: in a stone wall, stone is still a noun (*a very stone wall, *stonier).

Who heads "the dog"? Descriptive grammars and the early chapters of Carnie treat the dog as an NP headed by the noun. Under the DP hypothesis (Radford, later Carnie) the determiner is the head: [DP the [NP dog]]. The drill uses NP with the noun as head.

Two senses of "complement". In X-bar theory (and H&P), a complement is any dependent the head selects, including objects. In Quirk's clause analysis, "complement" means only the subject and object complements Cs and Co. Check which one an exam question means.

Constituency tests

Framework: neutral (Carnie, Radford)

A constituent is a word or string of words that the grammar treats as a unit. In a tree, a constituent is everything one node dominates, and nothing else. The tests below all use the same sentence: The student put the old books on the top shelf.

1. Substitution (pro-form)

Replace the string with one word: a pronoun for an NP, there/then for a PP, do so for a VP, one for an N′. She put them there. So [the student], [the old books] and [on the top shelf] are constituents.

Limits: pronouns replace whole NPs only (*the them), and some categories have no handy pro-form.

2. Movement (topicalisation)

On the top shelf, the student put the old books. vs *Top shelf, the student put the old books on the.

Limits: fronting is stylistically marked and fails for independent reasons: single function words (the) cannot move, and a fronted VP needs an auxiliary left behind (...and [put the books on the shelf] she did).

3. Clefting

It was the old books that the student put on the top shelf. / It was on the top shelf that...

Limits: VPs cannot be clefted (*It was put the books on the shelf that she did); APs only marginally.

4. Pseudoclefting (wh-cleft)

What the student put on the top shelf was the old books. / What the student did was put the old books on the top shelf. (works for the VP)

Limits: needs a fitting wh-word; PPs often sound odd.

5. Coordination

The student put [the old books] and [the new magazines] on the top shelf. Only like constituents coordinate.

Limits: the weakest test, it overgenerates. Non-constituents coordinate too: She gave [Mary a book] and [Sue a pen]; [Anna likes] and [Ben hates] opera.

6. Fragment (stand-alone) answer

What did the student put on the top shelf? — The old books. / Where? — On the top shelf. But not *The old.

Limits: the question has to exist; VP answers need a do-question (What did she do? — Put the books away.).

7. Ellipsis (deletion)

The student will put the books on the shelf, and the teacher will too. The whole VP [put the books on the shelf] is deleted as one unit.

Limits: only some categories can be elided (VP after an auxiliary, the noun after a determiner, a clause after a wh-word: Someone called, but I don't know who).

The logic. Passing a test is evidence that a string is a constituent. Failing one test proves little, because a test can fail for reasons that have nothing to do with constituency. Use several tests and trust them when they agree.

Exam traps

  • Giving a semantic definition of a word class without saying why it fails. Always define by form and distribution.
  • "Adverbs end in -ly." Friendly, lovely, ugly are adjectives; fast, hard, well, soon are adverbs without -ly.
  • Nouns as premodifiers (a stone wall, a London bus) stay nouns: no grading, no very.
  • Particle or preposition? She looked up the word: particle, it can move (looked the word up). She looked up the chimney: preposition (*looked the chimney up).
  • One test is not enough, and coordination alone proves nothing.
  • German speakers: German articles show case and gender (der/den/dem), so phrase boundaries are often visible in the endings. English the shows nothing, so you need position and the tests.
  • German speakers: German adverbs and adjectives often look the same (schnell). English mostly distinguishes them (quick/quickly), so "it describes how" is not enough: check whether it modifies a noun (adjective) or a verb, adjective or clause (adverb).

Clause elements

Framework: descriptive (Quirk), H&P terms noted

Category says what a constituent is (NP, PP, clause); function says what it does in the clause (subject, object...). The two are independent: an NP can be a subject, an object, a complement or an adverbial (We met [last week]).

ElementTypical formsHow to spot itExample
S subjectNP, clause, dummy it/therebefore the verb in statements; agrees with the finite verb (she walks); inverts with the operator in questions; nominative pronoun; picked up in tag questions[The tall woman] left.
V verb (H&P: predicator)Quirk's "verb phrase": auxiliaries + main verbalways presentShe [has been reading].
Od direct objectNP, clauseafter V; becomes S in the passive; answers what?/who(m)?; accusative pronounShe wrote [a letter].
Oi indirect objectNPbefore Od; recipient or beneficiary; can usually be paraphrased with to/for after OdShe gave [him] a book.
Cs subject complementNP, AP, clauseafter a copular verb (be, become, seem, look, remain, get, turn); characterises S; no passiveHe became [a doctor].
Co object complementNP, APafter Od; says what Od is or becomes (Od + be + Co is true)They made him [angry].
A adverbialAdvP, PP, NP, clauseusually optional and movable; obligatory with some verbs (be + place, put, live)She arrived [late].

Obligatory or optional adverbial? Delete it. If the clause becomes ungrammatical (She put the vase *(on the table)) or the verb changes meaning (They live in Graz → They live means "they are alive"), the adverbial is obligatory and counts in the clause pattern. If nothing changes (The children laughed (loudly)), it is optional and does not count.

The pattern belongs to a verb in one sense, so one verb can appear in several: She got angry (SVC), She got a letter (SVO), She got him a coffee (SVOO), She got the children ready (SVOC), She got home late (SVA: home obligatory, late optional). Optional adverbials never count, wherever they stand.

Prepositional verbs. In She relies on her sister Quirk calls rely on a prepositional verb and her sister a prepositional object; such clauses behave like SVO (many can passivise: Her sister can be relied on). Some courses analyse the PP as an adverbial instead, so the drill avoids these. It also avoids gave a book to Mary: books disagree on the function of to Mary.

Non-finite clauses usually have no overt subject; it is understood from the main clause ([Walking home], I... = I was walking). They can have one: [The weather being fine], we went out; I want [you to stay].

A main (independent) clause can stand alone. A subordinate clause is part of another clause, the matrix (superordinate) clause, where it works as S, O, C or A, or as a postmodifier inside a phrase.

Three jobs for a subordinate clause

Nominal (complement) clauses

Do the work of an NP: S, Od, Cs, or complement of an adjective or noun. [That he lied] is obvious. I know [that you're busy]. I wonder [whether she'll come]. I asked [who had called]. She wants [to leave]. [Swimming] is fun. Quirk also counts nominal relative clauses: [What she said] surprised me.

Relative clauses

Postmodify a noun and contain a gap linked to it: the film [(that) I saw __]. Relativizers: who, whom, whose, which, where, when, that, or zero.

Adverbial clauses

Function as A: time (when, before, after, while), condition (if, unless), reason (because, since, as), concession (although, though), purpose (so that, to-infinitive), result, manner.

Restrictive (defining)Non-restrictive (non-defining)
Jobnarrows down which one is meantadds information about a referent that is already identified
Commasnonecommas (or a pause in speech)
Relativizerwho, which, that, or zero if the gap is not the subjectwho, which, whose; never that, never zero
ExampleMy brother who lives in Graz is a doctor. (I have more than one brother.)My brother, who lives in Graz, is a doctor. (I have one brother.)

A non-restrictive which can also refer to a whole clause: She passed, which surprised everyone (sentential relative clause).

Extraposition or cleft? Both begin with It is/was. Extraposition: the clause can go back to subject position (It is obvious that he lied → That he lied is obvious). Cleft: delete it was and that and a complete sentence is left (It was Anna that called → Anna called).

Exam traps

  • Od or Cs? He became a doctor: Cs (it describes the subject, no passive: *A doctor was become).
  • NP adverbials: We met [last week], She cried [all night]: NPs, but A, not O (no passive, they answer when?/how long?).
  • Counting optional adverbials in the clause pattern. The children laughed loudly is SV.
  • Quirk's "verb phrase" is just the verb group; the generative VP includes objects. Don't mix them in one answer.
  • Existential there is the subject, not an adverbial: Is there a problem? There's a problem, isn't there?
  • German: V2. German puts the finite verb second whatever comes first (Gestern habe ich ihn gesehen). English stays SVO after a fronted adverbial: Yesterday I saw him, not *Yesterday saw I him. English inverts only in questions, after negative or restrictive openers (Never have I...), with so/neither, and in a few fixed patterns (Here comes the bus).
  • German: verb-final subclauses. ...weil ich müde bin, but English keeps SVO: because I am tired, never *because I tired am.
  • German: case vs position. German can front the object and stay clear (Den Hund beißt der Mann). English nouns have no case, so position is the function: The dog bites the man means the dog bites. Only pronouns show case (he/him, who/whom).
  • German: nothing between V and Od. *I read yesterday the book → I read the book yesterday. English keeps verb and object adjacent.
  • German: questions and negation need do with lexical verbs: Do you know? not *Know you?; I don't know not *I know not.
  • German: commas. German puts commas round every relative clause. In English a comma makes the clause non-restrictive and changes the meaning, and that is impossible after the comma.
  • German: impersonal passive. Mir wurde geholfen has no subject; English needs one: I was helped. English allows prepositional passives German lacks: This bed has been slept in.

Trees

Framework: generative phrase structure and X-bar (Carnie, Radford)

A tree and a labelled bracketing hold the same information: [NP [D the] [N dog]] is an NP node with two daughters, D and N. Every word gets a category, every phrase has a head of its own kind, and the lines never cross.

TermMeaning
node, root, terminalEvery labelled point is a node. The root is the top node; terminals are the words.
dominateA dominates B if you can go from A down to B. Immediately dominate: A is B's mother, B is A's daughter.
sistersDaughters of the same mother.
constituentAll and only the words one node dominates.
c-commandA node c-commands its sister and everything its sister dominates. (Binding: herself needs a c-commanding antecedent.)
triangleShorthand: the phrase is there, its inside is not drawn.

From flat rules to X-bar

Early phrase-structure grammar uses flat rules:

S → NP (Aux) VP NP → (D) (AP+) N (PP+) VP → V (NP) (NP/PP/CP) (AdvP+) (PP+) PP → P NP

Flat rules cannot show that student of physics forms a unit inside the student of physics with long hair (the one with short hair replaces it, *the one of chemistry fails). X-bar theory adds an intermediate level X′ and gives every phrase the same shape:

Specifier rule: XP → (YP) X′ Adjunct rule: X′ → X′ (ZP) or X′ → (ZP) X′ Complement rule: X′ → X (WP)
  • Head X: the word that projects the phrase (X → X′ → XP, the maximal projection).
  • Complement: sister of X, daughter of X′. Selected by the head.
  • Adjunct: sister of X′ and daughter of X′. The rule can apply again and again (stacking).
  • Specifier: daughter of XP, sister of X′. In TP the subject; in CP a fronted wh-phrase.
  • Branching is binary; every phrase is endocentric (has a head of its own category).
  • T (tense) heads the clause: it holds a modal, to, or a tense affix (-ed, -s, null present) that is lowered onto the verb (affix hopping). C heads CP and holds that, if, whether, for, a silent C, or an auxiliary moved up from T (subject-auxiliary inversion = T-to-C movement).

Conventions differ. Many courses leave out non-branching bar levels (they draw [NP [D the] [N dog]] instead of [NP [D the] [N′ [N dog]]]), which the gallery mostly does. Older books call TP "IP" or "S". The DP hypothesis would make every NP below a DP.

How to draw a tree, step by step

  1. Give every word a category (N, V, D, P, Adj, Adv, T, C, Conj).
  2. Find the verb and its tense or modal; that gives you T and the VP.
  3. Build the smallest phrases first: heads with their complements (X′), then adjuncts (another X′), then specifiers (XP).
  4. Decide complement or adjunct with the tests (one, do so, order, stacking).
  5. Attach modifiers where the meaning says they belong; ambiguous strings need two trees.
  6. Check: each phrase has exactly one head of its own category, every node is binary in X-bar, no lines cross, every word is under a category label.

Exam traps

  • Complements and adjuncts at the same level. The complement is the head's sister; each adjunct adds a new X′ above it.
  • PP attached to the wrong node. "with the telescope as the instrument" = VP (V′) attachment; "the man who has the telescope" = NP (N′) attachment.
  • Forgetting T. In an X-bar tree the tense (or the modal) sits in T even when it surfaces on the verb.
  • Mixing frameworks: an S → NP VP tree in an X-bar exercise, or a ternary ditransitive where your course demands binary branching (VP shells).
  • Triangles are a shortcut. Use them only for parts the task does not ask you to analyse.
  • Movement without traces. Show where a moved element came from (t, coindexed) if your course uses traces.
