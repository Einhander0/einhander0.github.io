Semantics vs pragmatics

Semantics studies sentence meaning: what words and sentences mean in the language system, independent of who utters them, largely in terms of truth conditions. Pragmatics studies speaker meaning (utterance meaning): what a speaker means by uttering a sentence in a particular context, and how the hearer works that out.

It’s ten o’clock. Sentence meaning: the time is ten. Said by a parent to a teenager still on the sofa on a school night: “go to bed”. Said by a host to a guest who wants to catch the last train: “you should hurry”. Same sentence, different speaker meaning.

Grice split the total meaning of an utterance into what is said (sentence meaning with reference fixed and ambiguity resolved, so it can be true or false) and what is implicated (meant but not said). An implicature is that extra meaning; the symbol +> reads “implicates”.

The Cooperative Principle and the maxims

Grice (lectures 1967, published 1975): conversation works because participants assume each other to be cooperative. The Cooperative Principle, in paraphrase: make your contribution what is required, at the point where it occurs, by the accepted purpose of the conversation. It is spelled out in four maxims. They are not rules of good behaviour; they are assumptions the hearer uses to interpret what is said.

MaximWhat it asksFlouted: example and implicature
Quantity(1) Make your contribution as informative as is required for the current purpose. (2) Don’t make it more informative than is required.A reference for a philosophy job that only praises the candidate’s handwriting and punctuality. +> he is no good at philosophy.
QualitySupermaxim: try to make your contribution one that is true. (1) Don’t say what you believe to be false. (2) Don’t say that for which you lack adequate evidence.In a downpour: Lovely weather for a picnic. (irony) +> the weather is awful. Metaphor and hyperbole flout Quality too.
RelationBe relevant.A: Mrs X is an old bag. B: The weather has been delightful this summer, hasn’t it? +> careful, change the subject.
MannerSupermaxim: be perspicuous. (1) Avoid obscurity of expression. (2) Avoid ambiguity. (3) Be brief (avoid unnecessary prolixity). (4) Be orderly.Parents in front of their children: I veto I-C-E C-R-E-A-M. +> we mustn’t let the children understand.

Maxim hedges show that speakers are aware of the maxims: as far as I know, I may be wrong, but (Quality); to cut a long story short, as you probably know (Quantity); by the way, anyway (Relation); I’m not sure if this is clear, but (Manner).

TermWhat happensExample

Grice himself named four ways of not fulfilling a maxim: violating, opting out, being faced with a clash, and flouting. Infringing and suspending were added by Jenny Thomas.

Scalar implicatures and Horn scales

A Horn scale lists alternative expressions ordered by informativeness, ⟨strong, …, weak⟩, where the stronger one entails the weaker ones. If a speaker chooses a weaker term, the hearer reasons: had the stronger one been true, a cooperative speaker would have said so (Quantity 1). So asserting the weaker term implicates that the stronger one does not hold.

The “not all” reading. Semantically, some means “at least one, possibly all”, and or is inclusive. The upper bound (“not all”, “not both”) is an implicature, which is why it can be cancelled: Some of the students passed; in fact, all of them did is fine. Under negation the scale reverses: Not all of them passed +> some did. Numbers are often treated as a scale too (three +> not four), but whether numerals are lower-bounded or exact in their semantics is debated.

After Grice: neo-Gricean pragmatics and relevance theory

Horn (1984) reduced the maxims (apart from Quality) to two opposing principles. Q-principle (hearer-based): make your contribution sufficient; say as much as you can, given R. It produces upper-bounding implicatures like some +> not all. R-principle (speaker-based): make your contribution necessary; say no more than you must, given Q. It produces enrichment: Can you pass the salt? +> a request; I broke a finger +> my own.

Levinson (2000) has three heuristics for generalized implicatures. Q: don’t say less than is required (scalar and clausal implicatures). I (informativeness): say as little as necessary, and the hearer enriches to the most specific, stereotypical reading (and → and then, and therefore; if → if and only if). M (manner): a marked expression signals a marked situation (Bill caused the car to stop +> not in the normal way).

Relevance theory (Sperber & Wilson 1986/1995) replaces the maxims with one notion. Relevance is a trade-off: the more positive cognitive effects an input yields, the more relevant it is; the more processing effort it costs, the less relevant. By the communicative principle of relevance, every utterance comes with a presumption of its own optimal relevance, so the hearer follows the path of least effort and stops when the expected relevance is reached. Relevance theory separates the explicature (the explicitly communicated content, developed from the decoded logical form by disambiguation, reference assignment and enrichment: I haven’t eaten → I haven’t eaten today) from implicatures (implicated premises and conclusions: so I’m hungry). Much of what Gricean theory calls generalized implicature, such as and → and then, is explicature in relevance theory.

Exam traps

  • Flouting vs violating. Both break a maxim. Flouting is overt and meant to be noticed, so it creates an implicature (irony). Violating is covert and meant to go unnoticed, so it misleads (a lie, a true but misleading answer). A lie is never a flout.
  • Entailment vs implicature: one test. Add “but not P” or “in fact, not P”. Contradiction → entailment (#Lisa ate all the biscuits, but she didn’t eat any of them). No contradiction → implicature (Some passed; in fact, all did).
  • Some doesn’t mean “not all”. Its meaning is “at least one”; “not all” is a scalar implicature. Likewise or is inclusive in its semantics.
  • Scalar implicatures are generalized. Yule lists “scalar” next to “generalized”, but scalar implicatures are a subtype of generalized conversational implicature. On an exam, say both.
  • Conventional implicature isn’t conversational. It has nothing to do with the maxims and can’t be cancelled, but it also isn’t part of the truth conditions: She’s poor but honest is true if she is poor and honest, whether or not there is any contrast.
  • Not every relevance implicature is a flout. There’s a garage round the corner observes Relation. A flout of Relation is an obvious change of topic.
  • Tautologies (Boys will be boys, War is war) flout Quantity: they are uninformative by definition.

Austin: doing things with words

Austin (lectures 1955, published 1962 as How to Do Things with Words) started from a contrast. Constatives describe or state something and are true or false: The kettle is boiling. Performatives do something in being uttered and are not true or false but felicitous or infelicitous (happy or unhappy): I name this ship the Queen Elizabeth. I bet you ten euros. I apologise.

An explicit performative contains a performative verb, usually in the first person, simple present, indicative, active: I promise to come. A primary (implicit) performative does the same act without naming it: I’ll come. The hereby test: only performative verbs accept hereby: I hereby promise… works, #I hereby walk to work doesn’t. Formal notices allow passives and other persons: Passengers are hereby warned…

Austin’s felicity conditionsIf broken
A1 There is an accepted conventional procedure with a conventional effect. A2 The persons and circumstances are appropriate for it.Misfire: the act is not achieved at all, it is void. Breaking A = misinvocation (a guest “marrying” a couple); breaking B = misexecution (a vital part of the ceremony left out).
B1 The procedure is executed correctly and B2 completely.
Γ1 The participants have the required thoughts, feelings and intentions. Γ2 They actually behave accordingly afterwards.Abuse: the act is performed, but it is insincere or hollow (promising with no intention of keeping the promise).

The distinction collapses. Austin himself showed that constatives can be unhappy too (The present king of France is bald fails much as a misfire does; The cat is on the mat, but I don’t believe it is like an abuse), that performatives can be judged against the facts (a warning can be unfounded, a verdict wrong), and that no grammatical test separates them, since primary performatives lack the verb and any statement can be made explicit (I state that…). Conclusion: every utterance performs an act. He replaced the dichotomy with a general theory of three simultaneous acts.

ActWhat it isThe bull is about to charge.
LocutionaryThe act of saying something: producing a meaningful utterance with sense and reference (Austin’s phonetic, phatic and rhetic acts).Saying that the bull (that one) is about to charge.

IFIDs (illocutionary force indicating devices) signal the force: performative verbs, mood and word order, intonation, stress, particles such as please.

Searle’s felicity conditions

Searle (1969) recast the conditions as constitutive rules of each illocution, in four types. Worked for a promise and a request (S = speaker, H = hearer, A = the future act):

ConditionPromiseRequest
Propositional contentA future act A of S.A future act A of H.
PreparatoryH would prefer S to do A rather than not, and S believes so. It isn’t obvious that S would do A anyway.H is able to do A, and S believes so. It isn’t obvious that H would do A anyway.
SincerityS intends to do A.S wants H to do A.
EssentialThe utterance counts as S undertaking an obligation to do A.The utterance counts as an attempt to get H to do A.

A “promise” to do something H doesn’t want (I promise I’ll tell your parents) fails the preparatory condition, which is why it is heard as a threat.

Direction of fit. With words-to-world fit the words must match the world (a statement is right if the world is as described). With world-to-words fit the world is to be changed to match the words (a request is fulfilled when H acts). A short version: in a declaration S causes X; in a representative S believes X; in an expressive S feels X; in a directive S wants X; in a commissive S intends X. Searle also notes assertive declarations (an umpire’s Out!, a jury’s Guilty), which must match the facts and also make them official.

Direct and indirect speech acts

The three sentence types have a basic function. When form and function match, the speech act is direct; when they don’t, it is indirect.

FormBasic functionDirectIndirect
DeclarativeStatementThe museum opens at ten.It’s cold in here. (request to close the window)
InterrogativeQuestionWhere’s the station?Can you pass the salt? (request)
ImperativeCommand / requestPass the salt, please.Less common. Go on, have another biscuit is usually analysed as an offer rather than a command.

Searle (1975): in an indirect speech act the speaker performs a primary illocution (the request) by way of a secondary, literal one (the question). The hearer infers the primary force because the literal act refers to a felicity condition of the intended one. Typical routes to requests: asking about H’s ability (Can you…? Could you…?, preparatory condition), H’s willingness (Would you mind…?), stating S’s want (I’d like you to…, sincerity condition), or stating or asking about reasons (Why don’t you…? You ought to…). Such forms are conventionally indirect; hints like It’s cold in here are non-conventional.

Answering only the literal act (Yes, I can, and not passing the salt) is a joke or a snub. That shows hearers process the indirect force, not just the literal one.

Requests in English and German

Cross-cultural studies of requests (House & Kasper 1981; the CCSARP project, Blum-Kulka, House & Kasper 1989) found that, on average, German speakers chose more direct request forms and fewer softeners than British English speakers in comparable situations. Neither system is more polite in absolute terms: each is polite by its own norms. The problem is transfer.

  • Geben Sie mir bitte einen Kaffee is a normal, polite order in Austria. Its word-for-word English version, Give me a coffee, please, sounds brusque. The unmarked English form is conventionally indirect: Could I have a coffee, please? or Can I get a coffee?
  • Please on its own doesn’t turn an English imperative into a polite request to a stranger or a superior the way bitte can in German.
  • Sie müssen hier links abbiegen is neutral information; You must turn left here sounds like an order. English prefers you need to, you’ll want to, you have to.
  • I want… (from ich will) sounds demanding; I’d like… is the neutral form.
  • The other direction: English indirectness can strike German speakers as vague or roundabout. That is a difference in convention, not in sincerity.

Exam traps

  • Illocution vs perlocution. Illocution = what you do in saying (warn, promise, argue). Perlocution = the effect by saying (frighten, persuade, convince, annoy). Test: “In saying X, I warned him” vs “By saying X, I convinced him.”
  • Questions are directives in Searle’s system (attempts to get H to give information). They are not a sixth class.
  • Threats are commissives: S commits to a future act, even though the aim is to influence H. Predictions are representatives, not commissives: S commits to a truth, not to doing anything.
  • Misfire vs abuse. A person without authority “sentencing” someone = misfire (no sentence happens). An insincere promise = abuse (a promise was made, just a hollow one).
  • Declarations have no sincerity condition but need an institution and a speaker in the right role.
  • Direct/indirect is about form and function, not politeness. Close the door, please is direct, however polite. Could you close the door? is indirect.
  • Tense matters. I promised to come is a report (a representative), not a promise.
  • German speakers: translated directness (Give me…, You must…, I want…) is a very common pragmatic error, and it tends to be heard as rudeness rather than as a language mistake.

Deixis

Deixis (Greek “pointing”) covers expressions that can only be interpreted relative to the context of utterance: who is speaking, to whom, where and when. I’ll meet you here tomorrow means nothing definite until you know the speaker, the addressee, the place and the day. The default deictic centre (Bühler’s origo) is the speaker, here, now. Proximal forms point near the centre (this, here, now), distal forms away from it (that, there, then).

Fillmore distinguishes the gestural use, which needs the situation to be monitored (Not this finger, this finger), from the symbolic use, where knowing the setting is enough (This town is lovely). Deictic words also have non-deictic uses: generic you (You never know), here and there.

Person deixis

First person picks out the speaker, second the addressee; third person refers to non-participants. Plural we can be inclusive (speaker + addressee: Shall we dance?) or exclusive (speaker + others, not the addressee: a shop assistant’s We close at six). English doesn’t mark the difference; Tok Pisin does (yumi inclusive, mipela exclusive). Shifted uses exist: a nurse’s How are we feeling today? means “you”.

Spatial deixis

Demonstratives and adverbs contrast proximal and distal. Motion verbs are deictic: come and bring mean movement toward the deictic centre, go and take movement away. The centre can be the addressee: I’m coming! (said to someone calling you) moves toward the addressee’s position. That shift is deictic projection.

Temporal deixis

Deictic time words (today, last week) are calculated from the time of speaking; calendrical terms (Monday, 2026) are not deictic on their own. Tense is grammaticalised temporal deixis. Coding time (when the message is produced) and receiving time (when it is received) coincide face to face but split in letters, recordings and signs: I’m not here now on an answering machine uses receiving time; a note saying Back in ten minutes is useless because you don’t know its coding time.

Social deixis

Relational social deixis encodes the relationship between speaker and addressee or referent: T/V pronouns, honorifics, terms of address. Brown & Gilman (1960) analysed T/V in terms of power (non-reciprocal use: the superior says T, receives V) and solidarity (reciprocal T among intimates, reciprocal V among distant equals). Absolute social deixis uses forms reserved for certain speakers or addressees: Your Honour, Mr President.

Discourse deixis vs anaphora

Discourse deixis points to a stretch of the discourse itself: That was the funniest joke I’ve ever heard (the joke just told), Listen to this: (the story about to come), this chapter. The former and the latter pick out referents by their position in the text, so they combine discourse deixis with anaphora. Anaphora is different: an anaphoric expression picks up a referent already introduced by an antecedent (A woman came in. She sat down.), whereas a deictic expression finds its referent in the situation (Who is she?, pointing). The same word can do either.

Deictic projection

Speakers can shift the deictic centre to the addressee or to someone else: Can I come in? (from outside the door, the addressee’s space as centre), I’ll come to your party, and in narrative (Now she was finally alone, past tense with a character’s “now”).

Presupposition

A presupposition is something taken for granted by an utterance: background information the speaker treats as already shared, as opposed to what is asserted. The symbol >> reads “presupposes”. Kim stopped smoking asserts that Kim doesn’t smoke now and presupposes that Kim used to smoke.

Constancy under negation is the standard test: a presupposition survives when the sentence is negated, an entailment doesn’t. Kim didn’t stop smoking still >> Kim used to smoke; but the entailment “Kim doesn’t smoke now” is gone. Presuppositions also survive in questions (Did Kim stop smoking?), with modals (Kim may have stopped smoking) and in the if-clause of a conditional: the “family of sentences” test.

Factive vs non-factive. Know, regret, realise, be aware, be glad that presuppose their complement is true. Believe, think, claim presuppose nothing about it. Pretend, dream, imagine (Yule’s “non-factive presupposition”) suggest the complement is false: He pretends to be ill >> he isn’t ill. Yule also groups triggers into existential, factive, lexical, structural and counterfactual presuppositions.

Projection and cancellation

The projection problem asks how the presuppositions of parts behave in a complex sentence. Karttunen (1973) sorted the environments. Holes let them through (negation, questions, modals: It’s possible that Kim stopped smoking >> Kim used to smoke). Plugs block them (verbs of saying: Mia said that the king of France is bald doesn’t commit the speaker to a king). Filters let them through only sometimes: If Kim used to smoke, she has stopped doesn’t presuppose that Kim smoked, because the if-clause supplies it.

Presuppositions are defeasible: context or an explicit denial can cancel them, especially under negation. The king of France isn’t bald: there is no king of France. Kim didn’t stop smoking; she never smoked in her life. Sue died before she finished her thesis doesn’t presuppose that she finished it. Entailments can never be cancelled. And if a presupposition is new but uncontroversial, hearers simply add it to the common ground: accommodation (Lewis 1979). Sorry I’m late, my car broke down works even if you didn’t know the speaker had a car.

Exam traps

  • Presupposition ≠ entailment. Negate the sentence. What survives is presupposed; what disappears was only entailed. (In the positive sentence the presupposition is usually entailed as well.)
  • Stop presupposes the old state and asserts the change. Kim stopped smoking >> Kim used to smoke; “Kim doesn’t smoke now” is asserted.
  • Manage presupposes trying and entails success. Forget to presupposes intention or obligation and entails not doing it.
  • Russell: false. Strawson: neither true nor false. Don’t swap them.
  • Anaphora is not deixis. She with an antecedent is anaphoric; she with a pointing finger is deictic.
  • Come isn’t always “toward the speaker”. It is toward the deictic centre, which can be projected onto the addressee.
  • The previous section, the next chapter look temporal but point to parts of the text: discourse deixis.
  • German speakers: du/Sie is social deixis built into the pronoun. English you encodes no social distance, so English marks distance with other means (address terms, modal verbs, indirectness). English isn’t less polite for lacking a V pronoun.

Face and face-threatening acts

Brown & Levinson (1978, 1987) build on Goffman’s notion of face, a person’s public self-image. Every competent adult (their “Model Person”) has two face wants. Positive face: the want to be liked, approved of and appreciated, to have one’s wants shared. Negative face: the want to be unimpeded, free to act and free from imposition. Many acts intrinsically threaten face: these are face-threatening acts (FTAs).

Weightiness of an FTA x: Wx = D(S,H) + P(H,S) + Rx. D is the social distance between S and H (symmetric); P is the power of H over S; R is the culture-specific ranking of the imposition. The heavier the act, the higher-numbered the strategy: you can say Pass the salt to your sister, but you ask your professor for an extension with every negative-politeness device available.

Criticism. The claim to universality has been challenged: Matsumoto (1988) and Ide (1989) argued that Japanese politeness is more about discernment (wakimae), using the forms one’s social position requires, than about protecting individual negative face. Blum-Kulka (1987) found that hints are not always rated the most polite: conventional indirectness was, because hints lack clarity.

Leech and Lakoff

Leech (1983): a Politeness Principle alongside the Cooperative Principle: minimise the expression of impolite beliefs, maximise polite ones. It has six maxims.

MaximMinimise / maximiseExample
TactMinimise cost to other; maximise benefit to otherCould I just borrow a tiny bit of paper?
GenerosityMinimise benefit to self; maximise cost to selfYou must come and have dinner with us.
ApprobationMinimise dispraise of other; maximise praise of otherWhat a wonderful meal!
ModestyMinimise praise of self; maximise dispraise of selfOh, it was nothing, really.
AgreementMinimise disagreement between self and other; maximise agreementYes, true, but…
SympathyMinimise antipathy; maximise sympathyI was so sorry to hear about your cat.

Lakoff (1973): two rules of pragmatic competence, Be clear (essentially Grice’s maxims) and Be polite, which has three sub-rules: (1) Don’t impose (distance, formality); (2) Give options (deference, hesitancy); (3) Make the hearer feel good, be friendly (camaraderie). When clarity and politeness conflict, politeness usually wins.

Conversation analysis

Conversation analysis (CA), developed by Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson from the late 1960s, studies recordings of naturally occurring talk to find the orderly structures participants themselves orient to.

Turn-taking

Turns are built from turn-constructional units (TCUs: a sentence, clause, phrase or single word). The point where a TCU could be complete is a transition-relevance place (TRP), where speaker change may happen. Allocation rules at a TRP (Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson 1974): (a) if the current speaker selects the next speaker (by name, gaze or an addressed question), that person must speak; (b) if not, anyone may self-select, and the first starter gets the turn; (c) if nobody does, the current speaker may continue. The result is mostly one speaker at a time with minimal gap and overlap.

Overlap is simultaneous talk, usually brief and close to a TRP, because listeners project where a turn will end; an interruption starts well away from a TRP. Backchannels (mhm, yeah, right, nods) show that you are listening and that you are not claiming the floor; they aren’t turns.

Adjacency pairs

Two utterances that are adjacent, produced by different speakers, ordered as a first pair part (FPP) and a second pair part (SPP), and typed: a particular FPP makes a particular SPP relevant. Examples: question–answer, greeting–greeting, offer–acceptance/refusal, request–acceptance/refusal, complaint–apology (or denial), assessment–agreement/disagreement, farewell–farewell. After an FPP the SPP is conditionally relevant, so its absence is noticed.

Preferred seconds are unmarked: immediate, short, simple. Dispreferred seconds are marked by delay (pause, uh, um), a preface (well, oh), token agreement or appreciation (That’s very kind, but…), an apology, hedges and qualifications (I’m not sure…), an account (the reason), and a softened, often indirect declining component. Preference is a structural notion about the shape of turns, not about what people personally want.

A: Want to come to the cinema tonight? B: Oh, um, well, I’d love to, but I’ve got this report due tomorrow, so… maybe another time? (delay, preface, appreciation, account, softened refusal)

Pre-sequences and insertion sequences

Pre-sequences prepare the ground for an FPP: pre-invitation (Are you doing anything tonight?), pre-request (Have you got a minute?), pre-announcement (Guess what!), pre-closing (Okay… right then…). A negative reply to a pre-invitation spares both sides an outright refusal. An insertion sequence is a pair embedded between an FPP and its SPP: A: Could I get a large coffee? B: Oat milk or regular? A: Regular. B: Sure, that’s 3.40. (Q1 Q2 A2 A1).

There is a preference for self-repair (Schegloff, Jefferson & Sacks 1977): the speaker of the trouble source gets the first chance, and outright correction by others is comparatively rare and usually softened.

Across cultures: notes for a German-speaking student

  • How are you? is usually a phatic routine (Malinowski’s phatic communion: talk whose function is social contact, not information). It opens an encounter, even with a shop assistant, and the expected answer is short and positive: Fine, thanks, you? An honest health report misreads it. German Wie geht’s? between acquaintances is more often meant literally, which is why the English routine can strike German speakers as superficial, and why a German-style answer can sound like oversharing in English. Different conventions, not different sincerity.
  • Small talk (weather, the weekend, the journey) is a positive-politeness ritual in English-speaking settings: it builds rapport before business. Going straight to the point can read as cold; on the other side, long small talk can feel like time-wasting to someone used to getting down to business.
  • Directness. Comparative work (House and others) describes German discourse norms as tending toward more directness and focus on content, English norms toward more indirectness and attention to the relationship. Disagreement in English is usually marked as dispreferred (I see what you mean, but…; I’m not sure that’s quite right). A bare No, that’s wrong lands harder in English than its German equivalent may feel. These are tendencies, not rules: register, region and personality all vary.
  • British understatement runs on implicature: not bad can be high praise, quite good is lukewarm in British English (in American English quite means “very”), with respect introduces disagreement, I’ll bear it in mind often means “probably not”.
  • Sorry is used very widely in British English as a negative-politeness softener (Sorry, could I just squeeze past?), including in situations where a German speaker might not apologise at all.

Exam traps

  • Positive politeness ≠ being nice; negative politeness ≠ being rude. Positive politeness appeals to the want to be liked and included; negative politeness respects the want not to be imposed on.
  • Could you open the window? is negative politeness (conventional indirectness), not off record. Off record means the intent is deniable, as in a hint.
  • P is the hearer’s power over the speaker, P(H,S), not the other way round.
  • Preferred ≠ what the speaker wants. After self-deprecation, disagreement is preferred; after an accusation, denial is preferred.
  • Backchannels aren’t turns, and overlap isn’t automatically interruption.
  • Adjacency pairs need not be strictly adjacent: insertion sequences can come between the two parts.
  • More indirect isn’t always more polite (Blum-Kulka): hints can be rated less polite than Could you…?
  • Leech’s maxims come in pairs of perspective: Tact and Generosity are the same idea from the other’s and the self’s side; so are Approbation and Modesty.
