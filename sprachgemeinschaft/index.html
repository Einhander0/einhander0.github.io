Varieties of a language

Variety is the neutral cover term: a language, a dialect, an accent, a register and a style are all varieties. Sociolinguists use it precisely because "language" and "dialect" are loaded words.

Language or dialect?

The everyday test is mutual intelligibility: if speakers understand each other, they speak dialects of one language. It fails in three ways.

  • Politics overrides it. Norwegian, Swedish and Danish are largely mutually intelligible but count as three languages because there are three states and three standards. Mandarin and Cantonese are not mutually intelligible but are often called "dialects" of Chinese because they share a writing system, a state and a cultural identity.
  • It is a matter of degree, and often one-sided. Intelligibility can be asymmetric: Portuguese speakers are often said to understand Spanish better than Spanish speakers understand Portuguese. Familiarity and attitudes matter as much as structure.
  • Dialect continua. Neighbouring dialects are mutually intelligible all along a chain, but the ends are not (see below), so there is no non-arbitrary place to draw the line.

"A language is a dialect with an army and a navy." Popularised by the Yiddish linguist Max Weinreich (1945), who said he had heard it from someone in his audience. The point: what counts as a language is decided socially and politically, not linguistically.

Abstand, Ausbau and the roof (Kloss 1967)

The German linguist Heinz Kloss gave English sociolinguistics two German loanwords.

  • Abstand language ("distance"): a language because it is structurally so different from its neighbours that nobody could call it a dialect of them. Basque is the textbook case.
  • Ausbau language ("building out", elaboration): a language because it has been deliberately developed into its own standard, with its own spelling, grammar books and functions, even though it is close to its neighbours. Swedish, Danish and Norwegian; Slovak and Czech; Serbian and Croatian.
  • Dachsprache ("roof language"): a standard that serves as the written norm over related dialects that have no standard of their own. Standard German roofs the Swiss German dialects and Low German.

Accent vs dialect

An accent is variation in pronunciation only. A dialect differs in grammar and vocabulary as well. So RP is an accent, and Standard English is a dialect that can be spoken in almost any accent: a Glaswegian who says I haven't seen him speaks Standard English with a Scottish accent; I've no seen him is Scots dialect grammar (Trudgill's distinction).

Standard languages and standardisation (Haugen 1966)

A standard is not the "original" or most logical variety. It is a variety that went through a social process, which Einar Haugen modelled in four steps:

  1. Selection of a norm (often the variety of the political, economic or cultural centre: London and the East Midlands for English).
  2. Codification: fixing the form in dictionaries, grammars and spelling rules (Johnson's dictionary, 1755; the Duden; the Österreichisches Wörterbuch).
  3. Elaboration of function: extending it to all domains: law, science, administration, literature.
  4. Acceptance by the community as the national or "correct" variety.

Selection and codification concern the form; elaboration and acceptance concern the function. Sociolinguistics describes how the standard came about; it does not treat non-standard forms as errors (descriptive, not prescriptive).

Pluricentric languages: your own case

A pluricentric language has several national centres, each with its own codified standard (a term associated especially with Michael Clyne). English has British, American, Australian and other standards. German has German, Austrian and Swiss Standard German. Austrian Standard German is a full standard variety, codified in the Österreichisches Wörterbuch, not a dialect: a news anchor on ORF saying Jänner is speaking standard German.

The -lects

  • Idiolect: the language of one individual, with its own vocabulary, habits and quirks.
  • Sociolect: a variety associated with a social class or group rather than a region.
  • Ethnolect: a variety associated with an ethnic group, often with features traced to a heritage language (Chicano English; the Yiddish-influenced English of some New Yorkers).
  • Multiethnolect: a variety that grows among young people of many ethnic backgrounds in the same urban neighbourhoods: Multicultural London English, Kiezdeutsch in Berlin.

Register, style, genre, jargon, slang

  • Style: variation along a formality scale within one speaker, depending on context, addressee and attention paid to speech (going vs goin').
  • Register: a variety defined by the situation or field it is used in, rather than by who uses it: legal English, sports commentary, medical case notes.
  • Genre: a conventional type of text or speech event with a recognisable structure: a recipe, a sermon, a job interview.
  • Jargon: specialised vocabulary of a profession or activity (morpheme, allophone; stat, NPO in a hospital), opaque to outsiders. In linguistics it is a neutral term.
  • Slang: very informal, often short-lived vocabulary that signals in-group membership, especially among young people.

Frameworks differ. Holmes & Wilson tie style to the addressee and formality and register to occupational or activity groups; Biber & Conrad treat register as situational variation, genre as conventional text structure and style as aesthetic preference. Use your lecturer's definitions in the exam and say which framework you follow.

Dialect continua and isoglosses

In a dialect continuum each place understands its neighbours, but intelligibility drops with distance. The West Germanic dialects form one from Flanders to Austria; the state border between the Netherlands and Germany decides which standard sits on top, not where the dialects change.

An isogloss is a line on a map marking the boundary of one feature. Isoglosses rarely coincide exactly; a bundle of them is what dialectologists treat as a dialect boundary. The Benrath line (maken vs machen) separates Low German from Central and Upper German, the area of the Second (High German) Consonant Shift. In England, isoglosses for the FOOT–STRUT split (northern speakers rhyme put and putt) and for BATH (bath with a short vowel in the north) run roughly across the Midlands.

Prestige

  • Overt prestige: the status a variety has in society at large, openly acknowledged, usually belonging to the standard and to educated speakers. It is what people mean by "correct" or "good" English.
  • Covert prestige: the positive value that non-standard, local forms have within a group, for solidarity, local loyalty, toughness or masculinity, but which people rarely admit openly. Trudgill's Norwich men are the classic evidence.

Prestige is local. In New York City, pronouncing post-vocalic /r/ (car, fourth) carries overt prestige; in England, non-rhotic RP does. Same feature, opposite social value.

The linguistic variable

A linguistic variable is a unit with two or more realisations, its variants, that don't change the referential meaning but do carry social meaning. Variables go in round brackets, variants in square brackets (or slashes):

  • (ing): [ɪŋ] ~ [ɪn] as in walking vs walkin'
  • (r): [r] ~ Ø after a vowel, as in fourth floor
  • (h): [h] ~ Ø, h-dropping in house
  • (t): [t] ~ [ʔ] between vowels, as in butter

The linguistic variable is the dependent variable; social factors (class, style, gender, age, ethnicity, network) are the independent variables. Labov's principle of accountability: count every context where a variant could have occurred, not just the striking tokens. Variation is probabilistic: nobody uses [ɪn] all the time; groups differ in how often.

Indicators, markers, stereotypes (Labov)

The test for a marker is style: speakers shift it with formality. The test for a stereotype is overt comment. A stereotype can drift away from real usage: toidy-toid street for "thirty-third street" is a New York joke far more than a New York pronunciation.

Hypercorrection

Overshooting a prestige norm. Two senses:

  • Individual: using a prestige form where it doesn't belong. between you and I; a northern English speaker who learns /ʌ/ for cup and then says butcher with /ʌ/ too; adding /h/ to apple.
  • Statistical (Labov): a group using more prestige variants than the group above it in the most formal styles. New York's lower middle class overtook the upper middle class for [r] in word lists and minimal pairs (the crossover pattern), which Labov linked to linguistic insecurity.

Language change in progress

  • Change from above: above the level of conscious awareness. Often introduces a prestige form, appears first in careful styles. Post-war r-pronunciation in New York.
  • Change from below: below awareness, starting in the vernacular, usually led by groups in the middle of the class hierarchy (lower middle and upper working class). Martha's Vineyard centralisation; the Northern Cities Shift. Above and below refer to awareness, not social class.
  • Apparent time: compare age groups at one moment and read the differences as change, assuming speech stays largely stable after adolescence. Cheap, but it can confuse change with age grading, where people change their usage as they age in every generation (teenagers use a form, adults drop it) and the community as a whole stays the same.
  • Real time: compare data from two dates. A panel study re-records the same speakers; a trend study samples the same community again with new speakers (e.g. replications of the department store study decades later).

Gender patterns, and the critique of them

Labov's generalisations from many urban studies:

  • In stable variation, men use more non-standard variants than women (Norwich (ing)).
  • In change from above, women adopt the incoming prestige form faster than men.
  • In change from below, women are usually the innovators.
  • The gender paradox: women conform more closely than men to overtly prescribed norms, but less closely than men when the norms are not overtly prescribed.

Critique. The explanations offered (women are more status-conscious, are "guardians" of the standard, must signal status through speech) are speculative. Early studies often assigned women their husband's or father's class. Sex was treated as a fixed binary category rather than gender as social practice. Patterns reverse locally (Milroy's young women in the Clonard; Eckert's burnout girls), and network, job type and community of practice often explain more than sex. For gender and conversation: the deficit model (Lakoff's "women's language" as weaker), the dominance model (talk reflects men's power, e.g. interruptions) and the difference model (Tannen's two cultures), later followed by social-constructionist approaches that treat gender as something people do in talk.

World Englishes: Kachru's three circles (1985)

Critique. The model classifies countries by history and status, not speakers by proficiency. It struggles with South Africa or Singapore, where many people now have English as a first language; a highly fluent Dutch or Swedish speaker is "Expanding circle" while some Outer circle speakers have little English; and it hides variation inside countries.

English as a lingua franca (ELF): English used between speakers who don't share a first language, most of them non-native speakers (who now far outnumber native speakers). ELF research judges success by intelligibility, not closeness to native norms: Jenkins's Lingua Franca Core lists the pronunciation features that matter for mutual intelligibility. A major ELF corpus, VOICE (Vienna-Oxford International Corpus of English), was compiled under Barbara Seidlhofer at the University of Vienna.

African American English: rule-governed, not "broken"

African American English (AAE; also AAVE, African American Language) is a systematic variety with its own grammar. Labov's work in the 1960s showed its rules are as regular as those of any variety, against the "verbal deprivation" (deficit) view.

Other features: negative inversion (Can't nobody beat him), completive done (I done told you), existential it (It's a man at the door). Its origin is debated between the Anglicist view (from British settler dialects) and the creolist view (from an earlier plantation creole). AAE became a public issue in the 1979 Ann Arbor "King" case and the 1996 Oakland "Ebonics" resolution, after which the Linguistic Society of America affirmed that AAE is systematic and rule-governed.

Exam traps

  • Accent ≠ dialect. RP is an accent. Standard English is a dialect and can be spoken with any accent.
  • Everybody speaks a dialect, including speakers of the standard. German Dialekt / Mundart in everyday use means rural, non-standard speech, so German speakers tend to use "dialect" too narrowly. Austrian Standard German is a standard, not a dialect.
  • "High German" is a false friend. In dialectology, High German (Hochdeutsch) means the southern (Central and Upper) German dialects that went through the Second Consonant Shift, as opposed to Low German. It does not mean "the standard" or "posh German".
  • Above / below = awareness, not upper or lower class.
  • Covert prestige doesn't mean the form is hidden; it means its value is recognised within a group but not publicly acknowledged. In Trudgill's self-report test, women over-reported (claimed more standard forms than they used) and men under-reported (claimed more local forms). Don't swap them.
  • Prestige is local: [r] is the prestige form in New York, r-lessness in RP.
  • Variable vs variant: (ing) is the variable; [ɪn] is one variant. Round vs square brackets.
  • Marker vs indicator: only markers shift with style. Stereotypes are what people comment on.
  • Apparent time is an inference; age grading can fake it. Real-time data checks it.
  • Kachru's circles are about countries and their history, not about how well individuals speak. Don't put South Africa or Singapore neatly into one circle without comment.
  • AAE copula absence is not random dropping: it occurs only where Standard English can contract (He's tall → He tall), never with am, in the past, or clause-finally (*That's what he).
  • Lakoff's "women's language" was a set of hypotheses from introspection. "Women use more tag questions" is not an established finding: some later counts found men using more, and many women's tags are facilitative.

Multilingualism and contact

Most of the world's speakers use more than one language. This tab covers how individuals and societies divide languages up, what happens when languages mix, how languages are lost and born, and how states try to steer all of it.

Kinds of bilingualism

  • Individual vs societal: a person who uses two languages vs a community or state where two languages are used. A bilingual state can have mostly monolingual citizens.
  • Simultaneous vs sequential: both languages from birth vs a second language added later (often defined as after about age 3).
  • Additive vs subtractive (Lambert): the second language is added without loss of the first (usually when the first has status), or it gradually replaces the first (typical of minority and immigrant languages).
  • Balanced vs dominant: roughly equal ability in both vs one stronger language. Truly balanced bilinguals are rare; ability often differs by domain.
  • Receptive (passive) bilingualism: understanding a language without speaking it, typical of the grandchildren in a shifting community.
  • Heritage speakers: people who learned a minority language at home but became dominant in the majority language.

Diglossia (Ferguson 1959)

A stable situation in which two varieties of the same language have complementary functions: a High (H) variety, learnt at school, used for formal and written purposes, and a Low (L) variety, learnt at home, used for everyday talk. Nobody uses H for ordinary conversation. Ferguson's four defining cases: Arabic (Classical/Standard vs colloquial), Swiss German (Standard German vs Schweizerdeutsch), Modern Greek (Katharevousa vs Dhimotiki) and Haitian (French vs Haitian Creole).

Ferguson's features: function, prestige (H is "superior", even "more beautiful"), literary heritage (H), acquisition (L at home, H at school), standardisation (H codified), stability (centuries), grammar (L has simpler inflection), lexicon (paired words, H learned and L everyday), phonology.

Fishman's extended diglossia (1967)

Fishman widened diglossia to any functional split, including two unrelated languages (Spanish H and Guaraní L in Paraguay), and separated individual bilingualism from societal diglossia:

Fishman also introduced domains (family, friendship, religion, education, employment): clusters of setting, participants and topic that call for one language: "who speaks what language to whom and when".

Code-switching, code-mixing, borrowing

Code-switching is a bilingual's alternation between two languages or varieties within one conversation. Poplack's (1980) structural types:

  • Tag switching: a tag or fixed phrase from the other language: Ich hab das schon erledigt, you know?
  • Inter-sentential: at a sentence boundary: I'm so tired. Ich geh jetzt schlafen.
  • Intra-sentential: inside a sentence or clause: Kannst du mir the red one geben? Needs the most proficiency in both grammars.

Poplack's constraints: switches favour points where the word orders of both languages match (equivalence constraint) and avoid falling between a bound morpheme and its stem (free morpheme constraint). Myers-Scotton's Matrix Language Frame model says one language supplies the grammatical frame and the other contributes content morphemes; her Markedness Model explains choices as unmarked (expected) or marked (making a point).

Borrowing: a word that has become part of the receiving language, adapted to its sounds and grammar and used by monolinguals (das Meeting, die Meetings; shoppen). The line between a single-word switch and a nonce borrowing is genuinely disputed. Code-mixing is used inconsistently: as a synonym for code-switching, for intra-sentential switching, or for word-level mixing. Say which sense you mean.

Functions (Blom & Gumperz 1972): situational switching follows a change in the situation (setting, participants, activity); metaphorical switching happens within one situation and borrows the associations of the other code (official vs local, distant vs warm). Gumperz later spoke of conversational code-switching (quotation, emphasis, addressee specification...).

Language shift, maintenance and death

Language shift: a community gradually gives up its language for another, typically over three generations (monolingual grandparents → bilingual parents → children who understand but answer in the majority language). Language death is the end point, when no speakers remain. Maintenance: the community keeps its language despite pressure.

  • Factors: economic opportunity, social status, urbanisation, intermarriage, schooling and media in the majority language, attitudes. Giles, Bourhis & Taylor's ethnolinguistic vitality sums them up as status, demography and institutional support.
  • Fishman's GIDS (Graded Intergenerational Disruption Scale): 8 stages from stage 8 (only a few elderly speakers left) to stage 1 (used in higher education, media and government). Stage 6, transmission to children at home, is the critical one.
  • Gal (1979), Oberwart, Burgenland: shift from Hungarian to German, led by young women who rejected peasant life; network type predicted language choice.
  • Dorian (1981), East Sutherland Gaelic: semi-speakers who understand fully but speak a reduced form, typical of the last stage before death.
  • Revitalisation: Hebrew (revived as an everyday spoken language and now native to millions), Welsh-medium schooling, Māori Kōhanga Reo "language nests".

Pidgins and creoles

  • Pidgin: a reduced contact language with no native speakers, arising when groups without a common language need to communicate for limited purposes (trade, plantations). Small vocabulary, little or no inflection, restricted domains.
  • Creole: a contact language that has become the first language of a community (nativisation), with a full grammar and vocabulary for all domains. Classic route: children of plantation workers acquire the pidgin as their mother tongue.
  • Lexifier (often called the superstrate): the language that supplies most of the vocabulary, usually the colonial one. Substrate: the languages of the less powerful groups, which tend to shape grammar and sounds.
  • Creole continuum: where the creole stays in contact with its standard lexifier, speakers command a range from the basilect (most creole, furthest from the standard) through the mesolect to the acrolect (closest to the standard). Guyanese "I told him": basilect mi bin tel am, mesolect mi tel im, acrolect I told him.
  • Decreolisation: the creole moves towards the standard over generations through schooling and contact.

Theories of creole genesis: monogenesis (one Portuguese-based ancestor, relexified) vs polygenesis; substratist vs universalist accounts, including Bickerton's controversial Language Bioprogram Hypothesis (children fall back on an innate grammar).

Language planning

  • Status planning: which language or variety is used for which functions: official language, national language, language of courts and schools. Swahili as Tanzania's national language; Bokmål and Nynorsk both official in Norway.
  • Corpus planning: changes to the language itself: codification, spelling and script reform, new vocabulary. The 1996 German spelling reform (Germany, Austria, Switzerland together); Turkey's switch to the Latin alphabet (1928); coining Hebrew words for modern life.
  • Acquisition planning: increasing the number of speakers: Welsh-medium schools, Māori language nests, adult courses.

Haugen's four standardisation steps map onto these: selection and acceptance are status-related, codification and elaboration are corpus-related.

Linguistic relativity (Sapir-Whorf)

The strong version, linguistic determinism, says language determines thought, so speakers cannot think concepts their language lacks. It is rejected (translation is possible, and Whorf's claims about Hopi time were later challenged by Malotki). The weak version, linguistic relativity, says language influences perception and memory; it has experimental support in some domains, e.g. Russian speakers' faster discrimination of light vs dark blue (two basic terms, goluboy and siniy) and absolute (north/south) spatial reasoning in Guugu Yimithirr speakers. Sapir and Whorf never wrote a joint "hypothesis"; the label came later.

Language attitudes

People judge speakers by their variety. Standard accents are usually rated high on status (competence, intelligence, education); local and non-standard accents often score higher on solidarity (friendliness, trustworthiness, humour). Methods: the matched guise technique (one bilingual or bidialectal speaker records the same text in two guises; Lambert et al. 1960), the verbal guise technique (different genuine speakers), direct questionnaires, and perceptual dialectology (Preston: non-linguists draw and label dialect areas on a map).

Exam traps

  • Ferguson's diglossia is about two varieties of one language with a stable functional split; H is never used for conversation. Two unrelated languages = Fishman's extended diglossia. Bilingualism by itself is not diglossia.
  • Personal letters are H in Ferguson's table; political cartoon captions and radio soap operas are L.
  • Switzerland vs Austria: German-speaking Switzerland is the textbook diglossia (dialect for conversation at every social level). Austria and southern Germany are usually described as a dialect–standard continuum, with colloquial forms in between, not classic diglossia.
  • Borrowing ≠ code-switching. Monolinguals use borrowings; only bilinguals switch.
  • Situational vs metaphorical: ask whether the situation (setting, participants, activity) changed. If not, it's metaphorical.
  • Pidgins have no native speakers. Names mislead: Hawaiian "Pidgin" is a creole; Tok Pisin (from "talk pidgin") now has many native speakers.
  • The lexifier gives the words, not the grammar. Haitian Creole is not "bad French".
  • Acrolect = top (Greek akros, highest), closest to the standard; basilect = base, furthest from it.
  • Status planning = function, corpus planning = form, acquisition planning = learners.
  • T/V: mutual Sie is solidarity semantic (reciprocal, non-intimate), not power. Only non-reciprocal use is the power semantic. English lost its T form (thou).
