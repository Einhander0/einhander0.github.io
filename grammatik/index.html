Tense and aspect

Descriptive grammar: Quirk, H&P

Tense is time marked by the form of the verb. English has exactly two tenses: past (walked, took) and present, better called non-past (walk(s), take(s)). There is no future inflection: will is a modal auxiliary like can or must. School books and ELT courses still say "future tense"; in a grammar exam say "will-future" or "future time".

Aspect is how the speaker views the situation in time. The progressive (be + -ing) presents it as ongoing, in progress, limited in duration. The perfect (have + past participle) places it before a reference point and makes it relevant at that point. Two tenses × four aspect combinations (simple, progressive, perfect, perfect progressive) give eight finite forms; add the four with will and you have the "twelve tenses" of ELT books.

Time is not tense. The present tense can refer to the future (The train leaves at six) and the past tense to the present or to nothing real at all (If I had a car..., I wanted to ask you something). The past form is best described as a remote form: remote in time, in reality (hypothetical) or socially (politeness).

Present perfect or past simple

The present perfect is a present tense with perfect aspect: it locates a past situation in a time period that reaches up to now. The past simple locates it in a period that is over, cut off from now. Four uses of the perfect (the standard labels go back to McCawley and Comrie):

Experiential

Have you ever been to Japan? I've read that book twice.

Something happened at least once in the period up to now; when is not important. Typical: ever, never, before, (three) times.

Resultative

I've lost my keys (so I can't get in). She's broken her arm.

A past event whose result still holds. If the result is gone (I found them again), use the past simple: I lost my keys yesterday.

Continuative

We've lived here since 2015. I've known her for years.

A state that began in the past and still continues. Needs since/for or how long. German uses the present here; English cannot.

Hot news

The Prime Minister has resigned. Police have arrested two men.

A recent event announced as new. Once the story goes on with details, the report switches to the past simple: He resigned at 9 a.m. after a meeting...

Definite past time blocks the perfect: *I have seen him yesterday. The adverbial places the event in a finished period, which contradicts the "up to now" meaning. The same goes for questions with when and for asking about details of a finished event: Where did you buy that jacket?

British vs American. American English often uses the past simple with just, already, yet and for recent events: Did you eat yet? I just saw him. Did you hear the news? British English traditionally prefers the perfect (Have you eaten yet?), though the American pattern is common in informal British speech. Neither is an error; a teacher should accept both and point out the difference. American English also uses gotten (has gotten better).

Perfect simple or progressive? Simple for a completed result or a quantity (I've written three emails), progressive for the activity and its duration (I've been writing emails all morning) or for visible after-effects (Why are you out of breath? I've been running). Stative verbs take the simple: I've known her for years, not *I've been knowing.

The German trap. The German Perfekt is a past tense in speech (and the main spoken past in Austria and the south), so it combines with gestern. The English present perfect is present-oriented. Ich habe ihn gestern gesehen = I saw him yesterday; Ich wohne seit 2019 hier = I have lived here since 2019.

The progressive and stative verbs

Dynamic situations happen, develop and can be in progress (run, write, melt). Stative situations simply hold (know, own, contain) and normally reject the progressive: *I am knowing the answer. Typical stative meanings: knowing and believing (know, believe, understand, mean, remember), feeling (like, love, hate, want, prefer), possession (have, own, belong), perception (see, hear, smell, taste), being and measuring (be, seem, consist, contain, cost, weigh).

Stativity belongs to a meaning, not to a verb. Many verbs have a stative and a dynamic sense, and only the dynamic one takes the progressive.

Other uses of the progressive: temporary situations (I'm living with my parents for now); changing situations (It's getting dark); annoying repeated habits with always, constantly (You're always interrupting); background to an event (I was cooking when the phone rang); politeness and tentativeness (I was wondering if...); personal arrangements (We're meeting at six).

Advertising and informal speech stretch the rule (I'm loving it, I'm wanting a change): stative verbs used dynamically to stress a temporary, intense state. Teach the norm, and explain these as marked.

Arrangement or intention? I'm going to see Anna (I have decided) vs I'm seeing Anna at six (we have arranged it). Often both are possible; the difference is one of emphasis, so avoid gap-fills that pretend only one is right unless the context fixes it.

Modality

Palmer's three types
TypeWhat it is aboutExamples
EpistemicThe speaker's judgement of how likely the proposition is to be true: certainty, probability, possibilityYou must be tired. It may rain. That can't be right. They should be home by now.
DeonticObligation, permission, prohibition imposed by a person, rule or authorityYou must wear a helmet. You may leave. You can't park here. You should see a doctor.
DynamicAbility or willingness of the subject (subject-oriented, not speaker-oriented)She can speak four languages. I'll carry that for you. The car won't start.

Terminology. Quirk et al. split modal meanings into extrinsic (= epistemic: about likelihood) and intrinsic (deontic and dynamic: human control). Others (Coates) say root for everything non-epistemic. Huddleston & Pullum use epistemic / deontic / dynamic like Palmer. Use your lecturer's set, and know the equivalences.

Formal properties of the central modals (can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would): no -s in the third person, no infinitive or participles (*to can → to be able to), followed by the bare infinitive (except ought to), and the NICE properties of auxiliaries: Negation with not, Inversion in questions, Code (ellipsis: Yes, I can), Emphasis (I CAN do it).

Modal-like expressions

  • must vs have to. In British English must tends to express the speaker's own authority (I must stop smoking), have to an external one (I have to wear a tie at work). American English uses have to for both; must is formal (rules, signs). In the negative they split completely: mustn't = prohibition, don't have to = no obligation. Must has no past: use had to.
  • be able to replaces can where a modal can't go: I'd like to be able to swim; I've never been able to. For one specific past success use was able to / managed to, not could: We were able to get tickets (couldn't is fine in the negative).
  • ought to = should, slightly more formal, rare in questions and negatives.
  • be allowed to (permission from others), had better (strong advice with a warning: You'd better hurry), be supposed to (expectation or rule, often broken: You're supposed to knock).

The subjunctive

  • Mandative subjunctive: the base form for all persons after verbs, adjectives and nouns of demanding or proposing (insist, demand, require, recommend, suggest, propose; essential, vital, important; the demand that). I insist that he be present. It is essential that she not be told. Normal in American English and formal British English. British alternatives: should + infinitive (that he should be present) or, informally, the indicative (that he is present).
  • Were-subjunctive: were for all persons in hypothetical meaning: If I were you; I wish she were here; as if he were the boss. Informal English uses was (If I was rich); If I were you is the fixed formula for advice.
  • Formulaic subjunctive in set phrases: So be it. Be that as it may. Come what may. Long live the King. Heaven forbid.

Exam traps

  • Calling will a "future tense" in a descriptive-grammar exam. English has two tenses; will is a modal. Say "future time" or "will-future".
  • Defining the present perfect as "a past tense". It is a present tense with perfect aspect; that is exactly why definite past adverbials clash with it.
  • Listing think, have, see, be as "verbs that never take the progressive". Stativity is a property of a meaning: I'm thinking of moving, She's being difficult.
  • Treating must and have to as identical. They part ways in the negative (mustn't vs don't have to) and in the source of the obligation.
  • Mixing up needn't have done (did it, unnecessarily) and didn't need to do (no necessity; usually not done).
  • Marking American Did you eat yet? or I just saw her as wrong. It is a variety difference.
  • German speakers: Perfekt ≠ present perfect (gestern takes the past simple); seit + present → present perfect with since/for; muss nicht = don't have to, never mustn't; werden suggests one "future tense"; standard German has no progressive, so learners both drop it (What do you do? for an action now) and overuse it once taught (I am knowing).

Conditionals

The key distinction is real (open: the condition may or may not be fulfilled) vs unreal (hypothetical or counterfactual). Unreal conditions use a backshifted, "remote" verb form: one step further into the past than the time meant.

Typeif-clauseMain clauseTime and realityExample
ZeropresentpresentAlways true: whenever A, then B (laws, habits)If you heat ice, it melts.
Firstpresentwill / modal / imperativeReal, possible futureIf it rains, we'll stay in.
Secondpast (were)would + VUnreal present or unlikely futureIf I had a car, I'd drive. If I won, I'd buy a farm.
Thirdpast perfectwould have + V-enUnreal past: the condition did not happenIf she had studied, she would have passed.
Mixed (past → present)past perfectwould + VUnreal past condition, present resultIf I had taken that job, I'd be rich now.
Mixed (present → past)pastwould have + V-enUnreal permanent condition, past resultIf I spoke Japanese, I'd have understood them.

Beyond the four formulas. Real conditions combine tenses freely: If you've finished, you can go. If he said that, he was wrong. If she's arriving at six, she'll need a lift. Teach the reality logic, not only the numbered types.

  • will/would in the if-clause is possible with special meanings: willingness (If you'll wait here, I'll fetch her), insistence, a result that comes first (If it will help, I'll come), politeness (If you would follow me...). Otherwise no will/would after if.
  • unless = except if, for real conditions: Unless you hurry, you'll miss it. It doesn't work for an unreal or emotional "if not": I'd be sad if you didn't come ≠ *unless you came.
  • provided / providing (that), as long as, on condition that: a strong condition (only if). even if: the result holds anyway. suppose / supposing: imagined situations. otherwise: if not. but for + noun: But for your help, we'd have failed.
  • in case ≠ if. Take an umbrella in case it rains = as a precaution, because it might rain (take it now). Take an umbrella if it rains = only if it is actually raining.
  • Inversion (formal) replaces if only with three auxiliaries: Had I known, I'd have called. Should you need help, call me. Were she to resign, we'd be in trouble. Never with other verbs: *Would I have time..., *Did I know...

The passive

Form: be + past participle, in every tense and aspect: is made, is being made, has been made, was made, had been made, will be made, must be made. Only transitive verbs passivise: the object of the active becomes the subject. The agent (by-phrase) is optional and usually left out: in corpus counts, roughly four in five passives have no agent.

Why use it

To keep the topic (given information) as subject and put new or heavy information at the end; when the agent is unknown, obvious or unimportant; to sound impersonal and objective (academic writing, reports).

Information structure, not "formality", is the main reason.

get-passive

He got arrested. She got promoted. The vase got broken.

Informal, always dynamic (an event, never a state), often for things that are bad or good for the subject, sometimes implying the subject's own involvement.

Prepositional passive

This bed has been slept in. The problem was dealt with. She was laughed at.

The object of a preposition becomes subject; works when the subject is affected or characterised by the action.

Double-object verbs

They gave me a book → I was given a book (most usual) / A book was given to me.

Only verbs that really take two objects (give, send, show, tell, offer, teach, lend). Not explain, describe, suggest: *I was explained the rule.

  • Verbs that don't passivise: intransitives (happen, arrive, die) and some transitive verbs of state or measure: have (possess), lack, resemble, suit, fit (be the right size), cost, weigh (measure): *Two brothers are had by her, *Ten euros was cost by the ticket.
  • Dynamic vs stative passive: The shop is closed at six (event: someone closes it) vs The shop is closed (state: it is shut; the participle is close to an adjective). The window was broken can be either.
  • Reporting passives: It is said that he is rich / He is said to be rich; She is believed to have left the country.
  • German speakers: the passive auxiliary is werden, so learners write *The church became built. English: be (or get).

Causative have and get

  • have something done: someone else does it for you (I had my hair cut; We're having the kitchen painted) or something happens to you (She had her bag stolen).
  • get something done: more informal; can suggest effort (I finally got the fridge repaired).
  • have someone do (instruct, mainly American: I'll have my assistant call you), get someone to do (persuade: I got my brother to help me), make someone do (force), let someone do (allow).
  • German speakers: sich die Haare schneiden lassen → *I let my hair cut. English: I had / got my hair cut.

Backshift is optional when the content is still true or still relevant at the time of reporting (She said she lives in Graz, if she still does; He told us the earth goes round the sun), and it does not happen at all after a present reporting verb (She says she's tired). Keeping the present signals that the reporter accepts the content as still valid.

The preposition to. In look forward to, be/get used to, object to, when it comes to, in addition to, to is a preposition and takes -ing. Test: can a noun go there? I look forward to the holidays → to seeing you.

Exam traps

  • Teaching conditionals only as four numbered formulas. Explain real vs unreal and the backshift "distance" logic; mixed and free combinations then make sense.
  • "Never will/would after if": true for plain future and hypothesis, false for willingness and politeness (If you would wait here...).
  • Treating unless as a perfect synonym of if not, and in case as a synonym of if.
  • Presenting backshift as obligatory. It is optional for still-true content, and it never applies after a present reporting verb.
  • Claiming the passive "needs" a by-agent, or that it exists for formality. Most passives are agentless and the motive is information structure.
  • Giving stop/remember/try as "both forms, same meaning". The meaning changes.
  • German speakers: werden-passive → *became built; lassen → *let my hair cut; dass-clauses → *want that you come, *suggest you to; zu-infinitive everywhere → *avoid to answer, *look forward to see; German subordinate clauses have their own word order, so learners copy direct-question order into indirect questions (*where is the station).

Count and non-count nouns

Count nouns have a plural and take a/an, many, few; non-count (mass) nouns have no plural and take much, little, and a piece / an item / a bit of to count. Countability is a property of how a noun is used: coffee (the drink) / a coffee (a cup); experience (knowledge) / an experience (an event); paper / a paper (an article); hair / a hair; work / a work of art.

Plural-only nouns (pluralia tantum): trousers, jeans, glasses, scissors, pyjamas, clothes, goods, thanks, outskirts; counted with a pair of where they have two parts. Plural collectives: police, people, cattle always take a plural verb. -s but singular: news, mathematics, physics, athletics.

Determiners and quantifiers

  • some and any, beyond the school rule ("some in statements, any in questions and negatives"). Some assumes a positive answer or a real amount: offers and requests (Would you like some tea? Could I have some water?). Any in affirmatives means "it doesn't matter which, no limit": Any bus will take you there. Call me any time. Stressed SOME = not all (SOME people liked it); some + singular count = an unknown one (Some idiot parked in front of my garage). Any after words with negative meaning: hardly any, without any, He denied any involvement.
  • much / many / a lot of. Much and many are normal in questions and negatives, after so, too, as (too much noise), and in formal writing (Much has been written about this). In informal affirmatives a lot of / lots of is the neutral choice: We have a lot of time, not ?We have much time.
  • few / a few, little / a little. A few friends = some (positive: I'm not lonely); few friends = not many (negative, rather formal). Same with a little / little + non-count.
  • each vs every. Each: two or more, one by one, can stand alone (each of them, they each got one). Every: three or more, all together, needs a noun (every student, every one of them, never *every of); every two weeks, almost every. Both + singular noun and verb.
  • both, either, neither for two: both (the / of the) brothers; either day is fine; neither of them is (formal) / are (informal).
  • most / most of the / the most. Most people (in general), most of the people here (a defined group), the most = superlative (who has the most money). German die meisten → *the most people.

  • whose for people and things: a writer whose books I love; a house whose roof leaks.
  • Prepositions: informal stranding (the woman (who/that) I spoke to) or formal fronting (the woman to whom I spoke); never *to who, never *to that.
  • Sentential relative: which referring to a whole clause, always non-defining: She passed, which surprised everyone. German was.
  • what = "the thing(s) that" and has no antecedent: What I need is sleep. Not after a noun or everything: *everything what.
  • that vs which in defining clauses: American style guides prefer that; British English uses both. Both are standard.
  • Relative adverbs: the town where I grew up, the day (when) we met, the reason (why) I came. Reduced relatives: the man sitting there, a book written in 1850.

German speakers: German puts a comma before every relative clause, so the comma carries no meaning; in English it decides between two readings. German der/die/das, welcher work for people and things, hence *the woman which.

Information structure

English clauses tend to run from given (known) to new information. Two principles: end-focus (the new, most important information comes last, where the main stress falls) and end-weight (long, heavy constituents come last: I gave it to the woman who lives next door). Because English word order is fixed (SVO), it uses special constructions to rearrange information:

ConstructionWhat it doesExample
PassiveMakes the given element the subject; agent at the end or omittedThe house was built by my grandfather.

wh-cleft (pseudo-cleft)What-clause + be + focus at the endWhat I need is a holiday.

German speakers: German verb-second order lets any constituent go first with inversion (Gestern ging ich ins Kino). English inverts only after negative/restrictive adverbials, so *Yesterday went I is wrong, but Never have I... is right.

Question tags

  • Form: auxiliary (or do) + pronoun, normally with reversed polarity: positive clause → negative tag, negative clause → positive tag. She's been to Spain, hasn't she? You don't eat meat, do you?
  • Special cases: I'm right, aren't I? · Let's go, shall we? · imperatives: Open the window, will you / would you / could you? · Don't forget, will you? · There's a problem, isn't there? · Nobody came, did they? · Everyone's here, aren't they? · This is yours, isn't it? · You'd better go, hadn't you? · He used to live here, didn't he? · negative words make the clause negative: You never listen, do you?
  • Intonation: falling tag = the speaker expects agreement, asks for confirmation (It's cold, isn't it? ↘). Rising tag = a real question, the speaker is unsure (You're coming, aren't you? ↗).
  • Same-polarity tags (positive + positive) show interest, surprise or sarcasm: So you're the new teacher, are you?
  • German speakers: oder?, nicht?, gell? never change, so learners overuse one tag: *You're coming, isn't it?

Concord (subject-verb agreement)

  • Grammatical concord: the verb agrees with the head of the subject: The quality of the essays is high.
  • Notional concord: meaning overrides form. British English allows plural verbs with collective nouns seen as individuals (The team are playing well; the government have decided); American English normally uses the singular. A number of students are vs The number of students is; Ten euros is a lot; Fish and chips is my favourite.
  • Always plural: police, people, cattle. Singular despite meaning: everyone, somebody, each (+ singular verb, but they/their as pronoun).
  • Proximity: the nearest noun can win: Neither the teacher nor the students were.... There's + plural (There's two problems) is very common in informal speech but avoided in writing: There are two problems.

Exam traps

  • Repeating "some in statements, any in questions and negatives" as the whole story. Offers, requests and free-choice any break it.
  • Calling a noun countable or uncountable once and for all: a coffee, an experience, a paper.
  • the with generic plurals and abstract nouns: *The dogs are loyal animals, *The life is hard.
  • Saying which is wrong in defining clauses. It's a style preference (American), not a rule; that in non-defining clauses is the real error.
  • Forgetting that it-clefts and wh-clefts are about focus, and inversion after negative adverbials is about emphasis: you must be able to say why a writer chose them.
  • German speakers: article with abstract nouns (das Leben), no article with jobs (Er ist Lehrer), die meisten → *the most, plural Möbel/Informationen/Nachrichten, singular Polizei/Hose/Brille, commas before every relative clause, invariable tags (oder?).

Explaining grammar in class

Form, meaning, use (Larsen-Freeman's three dimensions). For every structure know: how it is built (have/has + past participle), what it means (a past situation connected to now), and when and why speakers choose it (news, experience; not with yesterday). Learners' errors can sit in any of the three: *I have went is form, *I have seen him yesterday is meaning, overusing will for plans is use.

Concept checking questions (CCQs) test whether learners have understood the meaning, without asking "Do you understand?". They are short, use simpler language than the target and have clear yes/no or either/or answers.

Model sentenceConcept checking questions (expected answer)
She has lived in Graz for five years.Does she live in Graz now? (Yes.) When did she start living there? (Five years ago.) Is it finished? (No.)
I used to smoke.Did I smoke in the past? (Yes.) Do I smoke now? (No.) Once or many times? (Many times.)
If I had a car, I'd drive to work.Do I have a car? (No.) Are we talking about the past? (No, now.) Do I drive to work? (No.)
If she had studied, she would have passed.Did she study? (No.) Did she pass? (No.) Past or present? (Past.)
When I arrived, the film had started.What happened first? (The film started.) Did I see the beginning? (No.)
She had her car repaired.Did she repair it herself? (No.) Who did? (A mechanic.) Who asked for it? (She did.)
You don't have to wear a tie.Is it necessary? (No.) Is it OK to wear one? (Yes.)

Timelines make time reference visible: the past simple sits in a closed section of the past; the present perfect reaches up to now.

Error or mistake? Corder's distinction: an error is systematic and reflects the learner's current interlanguage (they don't know the rule and can't self-correct); a mistake is a performance slip they can correct when it is pointed out. Interlingual errors come from the first language (interference, negative transfer: *I live here since 2019); intralingual or developmental errors come from English itself (overgeneralisation: *I didn't knew, *I am knowing). Errors that persist despite teaching may be fossilised.

Correcting: a recast (repeat the sentence correctly), elicitation (I ... ?), a metalinguistic clue (Yesterday: is that finished time?), a clarification request (Sorry?). Fluency activities: correct later; accuracy work: correct on the spot.
