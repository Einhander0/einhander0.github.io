Morphemes, morphs and allomorphs

A morpheme is the smallest unit of language that carries meaning or a grammatical function. unkindness has three: un- 'not', kind, and -ness 'state or quality'. Morphemes are not syllables: elephant has three syllables and one morpheme, cats has one syllable and two morphemes.

The morpheme is the abstract unit. What you actually hear or write is a morph. When one morpheme has several morphs depending on where it occurs, those are its allomorphs: the plural morpheme is /s/ in cats, /z/ in dogs, /ɪz/ in horses and -en in oxen; the negative prefix is in- in incomplete, im- in impossible, il- in illegal and ir- in irregular.

Notation. Morphemes are often written in braces, {PLURAL} or {-s}; allomorphs in slashes. Prefixes are written with a following hyphen (un-), suffixes with a preceding one (-ness). A word made of one morpheme is simplex (table); a word of several is complex (tables, unkindness).

The four-way classification

Two separate questions: can it stand alone as a word (free) or not (bound)? And does it carry content or grammar? The common teaching scheme crosses them like this:

TypeWhat it doesExamples
Free lexicalContent words: nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs. Open class, new members are added all the time.dog, run, happy, slow
Free functionalGrammar words: articles, pronouns, conjunctions, auxiliaries, prepositions. Closed class.the, a, she, and, because, of, in, will
Bound derivationalAffixes that build new words (new lexemes), often of a new word class.un-, re-, dis-, -ness, -ize, -ful, -able
Bound inflectionalAffixes that make grammatical forms of the same word. English has only eight, all suffixes.-s, -ed, -ing, -er, -est

What does not fit the boxes. Bound roots (-ceive, -mit) and cranberry morphemes (cran-) are bound but are roots, not affixes. Keep the two questions apart: the is free but functional; -ceive is bound but a root.

Root, stem and base

Take untouchables.

  • Root: the irreducible core that remains when every affix is stripped off and that carries the main meaning: touch.
  • Base: any form an affix attaches to. touch is the base of -able, touchable is the base of un-, and untouchable is the base of plural -s. Every root can be a base; not every base is a root.
  • Stem: the form that inflectional affixes attach to: here untouchable (used as a noun). A stem can already contain derivational affixes.

This is Katamba’s usage; Plag defines root and base the same way but avoids the term stem. Some books use stem loosely for any base. In German grammar Stamm is used in the same inflectional sense.

Where affixes go

Prefix

Before the base.

un-happy, re-write, pre-heat, dis-like

Suffix

After the base. All English inflection is suffixal.

kind-ness, modern-ize, cat-s, walk-ed

Infix

Inside the base. Tagalog actor-focus -um-: sulat → s-um-ulat. English has only expletive infixation: a swear word or intensifier goes in right before the stressed syllable: abso-bloody-lutely, fan-bloody-tastic, never *ab-bloody-solutely.

Since the inserted piece is a whole word, some argue it is not a true infix.

Circumfix

Two parts around the base that work as one unit. German participles: ge-spiel-t, ge-sung-en. English has no clear circumfix.

Two caveats. Possessive 's attaches to whole noun phrases (the King of England's hat = the hat of the King of England), so many modern grammars treat it as a clitic, not a suffix. And the past participle of regular verbs looks exactly like the past tense (walked): one form, two functions (syncretism).

Allomorphy: the -s and -ed rules

Plural -s, 3rd person -s and possessive 's all follow the same rule; so does past -ed (and the regular past participle). The choice depends on the last sound of the stem, not its spelling.

Many books treat /z/ and /d/ as the underlying forms, with two rules: insert a vowel between two similar sounds, then devoice after a voiceless sound. GA-oriented books write /əz/ and /əd/ for /ɪz/ and /ɪd/; same thing.

What conditions the choice?

ConditioningThe choice depends onExamples
Phonologicalthe neighbouring sounds; predictable by ruleplural /s z ɪz/, past /t d ɪd/, a vs an, in- / im- / il- / ir-
Lexicalthe particular word; must be memorizedoxen (-en), children (-ren, plus a vowel change), sheep (zero), feet (vowel change), criteria (Greek plural)
Morphologicala particular morphological context, e.g. a following suffixelectric /k/ → electricity /s/; sane /eɪ/ → sanity /æ/; decide /d/ → decision /ʒ/. The sounds alone don't trigger it: kicking keeps /k/ before /ɪ/, saneness keeps /eɪ/.

Terminology varies. Some books lump lexical and morphological conditioning together as "grammatical" or "morphological" conditioning. The contrast every course tests is the same: predictable from sounds or not.

Special cases

Suppletion

A grammatical form built on an unrelated stem: go / went, be / am / is / was, good / better / best, bad / worse. Some books call bring / brought or think / thought partial (weak) suppletion.

Internal modification

A vowel change inside the stem marks the category: sing / sang / sung (ablaut), foot / feet, mouse / mice (umlaut, historically).

Zero morph

A morpheme with no overt form, posited because of the pattern elsewhere: plural sheep, past hit, put. Conversion is sometimes analysed as zero-derivation for the same reason.

Cranberry morphemes

Bound, occur in one word only, no meaning of their own: cran- (cranberry), huckle- (huckleberry), luke- (lukewarm), -kempt (unkempt). We still count them as morphemes because the rest of the word is clearly a morpheme.

Bound roots

Latinate roots that never stand alone but recur with stable behaviour: -ceive (receive, deceive, conceive, with nouns in -ception), -mit (permit, submit, admit, with nouns in -mission). The shared alternation is the argument that they are real morphemes.

Empty morphs (debated)

Material with no meaning that belongs to no morpheme, like the linking -o- in speedometer or -i- in pesticide. Others assign it to a neighbouring allomorph instead, so whether empty morphs exist is a matter of analysis.

Portmanteau morph

One morph, several morphemes at once (cumulative exponence): -s in walks = 3rd person + singular + present; French au = à + le. Not the same as a blend ("portmanteau word").

Exam traps

  • Counting letters or syllables. butter is not butt + -er, sing contains no -ing, table contains no -ble. A morph must recur with the same meaning in other words.
  • Same form, different morpheme. -er: agent (teach-er, derivational), comparative (tall-er, inflectional), nothing (butter). -en: participle (eat-en), verb-forming (dark-en), adjective-forming (wood-en), plural (ox-en). -ed: past (walk-ed) vs adjective from a noun (talent-ed, blue-eyed). -ing: progressive (is build-ing) vs noun (a new build-ing).
  • -ly is not always adverbial. friendly, lovely, costly are adjectives made from nouns.
  • Free ≠ lexical. the is free and functional; -ceive is bound and a root.
  • Sound, not spelling. laughed ends in /t/ (gh = /f/), boxes takes /ɪz/ because x = /ks/, months takes /s/ after /θ/, breathes takes /z/ after /ð/.
  • German final devoicing. Plural /z/ in dogs and past /d/ in begged stay lenis, and the vowel before them stays long. Saying [dɒks] mixes up two allomorphs.
  • Inflection outside derivation. In trees and bracketings, -s, -ed, -ing go on last: [[teach-er]-s].
  • Possessive 's. Counted among the eight inflections in many introductions, but analysed as a clitic in others. Say which view you take.

Most English prefixes are class-maintaining; en- and be- are the classic exceptions.

Productivity and blocking

Productivity is how freely a pattern makes new words. -ness attaches to almost any adjective and new -ness words go unnoticed; -th (warmth, length) no longer makes new words. Corpus linguists (Baayen, Plag) measure it partly by hapax legomena: an affix with many words that occur only once in a corpus is actively being used to coin new ones.

Blocking: a possible word is not formed because an established word already has that meaning. *stealer is blocked by thief, *gloriosity by glory (but curiosity is fine: there is no *cury), *goed by went. Blocking is a strong tendency, not a law: scene-stealer exists.

Level ordering (lexical morphology, Siegel, Allen, Kiparsky) predicts that Level 2 affixes always sit outside Level 1 affixes: hope-less-ness is fine, *fear-less-ity is impossible. Famous counterexamples: govern-ment-al (Level 2 -ment inside Level 1 -al) and un-grammatical-ity. Plag and Hay explain ordering through each affix's own selectional restrictions and how parsable it is instead.

Some textbooks use copulative for both coordinative types; others keep dvandva and appositional apart. Follow your lecturer's labels.

Head. English compounds are right-headed (Williams' Righthand Head Rule): the right element fixes the word class (sky-blue is an adjective because blue is), the core meaning (houseboat is a boat, boathouse a house) and carries inflection (bookshelves). Exceptions: exocentric compounds, and lexicalized phrases like passers-by, mothers-in-law, attorney general.

Stress. Compounds usually stress the first element, phrases the second:

CompoundPhrase
a ˈgreenhouse (glass building for plants)a green ˈhouse (a house painted green)
a ˈblackbird (a species)a black ˈbird (any bird that is black)
a ˈblackboarda black ˈboard
the ˈWhite Housea white ˈhouse

The stress test has exceptions: apple ˈpie and Fifth ˈAvenue have phrasal stress although many treat them as compounds, while ˈFifth Street has compound stress.

Spelling is no criterion. English writes compounds open (truck driver, ice cream), hyphenated (sister-in-law) or closed (bookshelf), often inconsistently. Better tests: stress, a meaning that isn't just the sum of the parts, and that the first part can't be modified on its own (*a very greenhouse).

Recursion. A compound can contain compounds: [[[student film] society] committee].

German speakers: German writes every compound as one word (Lastwagenfahrer) and uses linking elements (Arbeit-s-amt). English writes many compounds as two words (truck driver, not *truckdriver) and has only fossilized linking elements (craftsman). Check a dictionary; don't glue English compounds together.

Conversion

The form stays the same, only the word class changes: noun → verb (to bottle, to email, to microwave), verb → noun (a run, a big ask), adjective → verb (to empty, to slow down), even preposition → verb (to up the price). The direction is decided by meaning (to bottle means 'put into a bottle', so the noun is basic), by history, and by frequency.

Some noun–verb pairs differ in stress: the noun is stressed on the first syllable, the verb on the second (capitals mark the stressed syllable). Whether these count as conversion is debated, since the form does change.

German has conversion too (das Laufen; Film → filmen, Google → googeln), but a German verb always carries an inflectional ending (-en / -n), so noun and verb never look identical the way English a bottle / to bottle do. That is why conversion is so much more visible in English.

German-speaker trap: pseudo-anglicisms

These look English but are German creations. Using them in English is a classic mistake.

Exam traps

  • Backformation vs clipping. Clipping cuts arbitrarily and keeps the meaning and word class (laboratory → lab). Backformation removes something taken to be an affix and usually yields a new word class (editor N → edit V).
  • Blend vs compound. A compound keeps both bases whole (breakfast room); a blend fuses fragments (brunch).
  • Acronym vs initialism. NATO is said as a word; BBC letter by letter.
  • Calque vs borrowing. kindergarten is borrowed (German form); worldview is a calque (English form, German pattern).
  • Conversion has a direction. Name it: bottle N → V.
  • Eponyms look like compounds. sandwich is not sand + wich; it is named after the Earl of Sandwich.

Morphological trees

A tree (or a labelled bracketing) shows the order in which a complex word is built. Each branching node joins exactly two parts (binary branching) and gets the word class of the result: N noun, V verb, A adjective, Adv adverb. Affixes carry no label of their own. The two parts under a node are its immediate constituents.

How to decide. Check what each affix attaches to (re- and reversative un- take verbs, negative un- takes adjectives, -able takes verbs, -ness takes adjectives), and whether every intermediate node is an existing or possible word. unhappiness = [[un-happy]-ness], because negative un- attaches to adjectives, not nouns; un- + happiness would wrongly mean 'not happiness'.

Structural ambiguity

One string, two structures, two meanings. unlockable contains two different prefixes un-.

Compounds can be ambiguous in the same way: student film society.

Bracketing paradoxes

Words whose meaning needs one bracketing but whose form (phonology or affix restrictions) needs another.

  • ungrammaticality: meaning [[un-grammatical]-ity] 'the property of being ungrammatical', but level ordering wants Level 2 un- outside Level 1 -ity.
  • nuclear physicist: semantically [[nuclear physics]-ist] (someone who does nuclear physics, not a physicist who is nuclear); morphologically nuclear modifies the word physicist. Same with transformational grammarian.

Morphological typology

Languages differ in how much they pack into a word (synthesis: morphemes per word) and how cleanly morphemes can be separated (fusion). The four classic types are idealizations; real languages mix them.

Isolating (analytic)

Words are mostly one morpheme; grammar comes from word order and separate words. Mandarin, Vietnamese, Thai, Yoruba.

wǒchīlefàn
IEATPFVRICE

Mandarin: 'I ate (the) rice'

Agglutinating

Several affixes per word, each with one meaning, clear boundaries. Turkish, Finnish, Hungarian, Japanese, Korean, Swahili.

ev-ler-im-de
HOUSEPLMYIN

Turkish: 'in my houses'

Fusional (inflecting)

One affix carries several meanings at once and may fuse with the stem. Latin, Russian, Ancient Greek, Sanskrit; German partly.

am-ō
LOVE1SG.PRES.ACT.IND

Latin: 'I love'. Root-and-pattern languages like Arabic (k-t-b: kataba 'he wrote', kitāb 'book') are sometimes split off as introflexive.

Polysynthetic (incorporating)

Very long words; one verb can hold subject, object and adverbial meanings, often by incorporating a noun. Inuktitut, Greenlandic, Mohawk, Chukchi.

qangatasuukkuvimmuuriaqalaaqtunga

Inuktitut, one word: 'I'll have to go to the airport'

Index of synthesis

English is mostly analytic: will go, more beautiful, the roof of the house. It keeps some fusion (-s in walks = 3rd person + singular + present; went, sang) and agglutinating derivation (un-help-ful-ness). Old English, with its case endings, was far more synthetic (index about 2.1).

Don't confuse polysynthetic with agglutinating. Turkish words are long chains of one-meaning affixes; polysynthetic words go further: a single word can hold what English says in a whole clause, either by incorporating noun roots into the verb (Mohawk, Chukchi) or by stacking many suffixes with word-like meanings on one root (Inuktitut, Greenlandic). Many polysynthetic languages are also agglutinating in technique.
