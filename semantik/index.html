Sense and reference

Frege (1892) split meaning in two. The reference of an expression is the thing in the world it picks out (the referent). Its sense is the way it presents that thing, the information it carries. The Morning Star and the Evening Star have the same referent, the planet Venus, but different senses. That is why The Morning Star is the Evening Star was a real astronomical discovery, while The Morning Star is the Morning Star tells you nothing.

Reference depends on the occasion of use: the president, I, today, my mother keep their sense but pick out different referents depending on who says them, when and where. Some expressions have sense but no referent at all: the present king of France, unicorn.

German-speaker trap. Frege wrote Sinn and Bedeutung. His Bedeutung is reference, not "meaning", and his Sinn is sense. Translating Bedeutung as "meaning" in an exam answer on Frege turns the distinction upside down.

Denotation vs connotation

Denotation: the core meaning that links a word to the things it applies to. Connotation: the extra associations, emotional or cultural, that come with it. slim, skinny and scrawny share a denotation (having little body fat) but connote approval, mild disapproval and strong disapproval.

Extension vs intension

Extension: the set of all things an expression applies to (every dog there is). Intension: the properties something must have to belong to that set. Two expressions can share an extension and differ in intension: creature with a heart and creature with kidneys pick out the same animals (Quine's example) but don't mean the same.

Reference vs denotation

In Lyons's terms, denotation is a stable relation between a lexeme and a class of things, independent of any utterance: dog denotes dogs. Reference happens in an utterance: in The dog is ill the speaker refers to one particular dog.

Terms overlap between books. Sense and intension are close cousins, and so are denotation and extension. Philosophers often use "denotation" for what linguists call reference. Use the pairs your lecturer uses and define them when you write.

Lexical (sense) relations

Tap a card for tests and examples. These relations hold between senses, not between word forms, so one form can be in several relations through different senses.

Synonymysame or nearly the same meaning: big / large+

Absolute synonyms would be interchangeable in every context with no change in meaning of any kind. They hardly exist: languages tend not to keep two words for exactly the same job. Real synonyms are near-synonyms that differ in at least one of these:

  • Dialect: autumn/fall, lorry/truck, flat/apartment.
  • Register / formality: buy/purchase, kid/child, hide/conceal.
  • Connotation: slim/skinny, die/pass away.
  • Collocation: a big mistake vs ?a large mistake; my big sister but not *my large sister; start a car but not *begin a car.

Test: substitute one for the other in many sentences. If the truth conditions never change, the words are (cognitive) synonyms. Mutual entailment: This is a big house ⇔ This is a large house.

Textbooks vary. Some reserve "antonymy" for gradable pairs only and call the others complementaries, converses and reversives (Lyons, Cruse). Some treat reversives as a kind of directional opposite rather than antonymy at all, and Saeed calls them "reverses". Comparatives of gradable antonyms are converses: bigger than/smaller than.

Markedness: in a gradable pair one term is usually neutral in questions. How old is the baby? doesn't suggest the baby is old; How young is she? suggests she is young. old, long, tall, wide are the unmarked members.

Hyponymykind-of inclusion: tulip → flower+

The meaning of the hyponym includes the meaning of the hypernym (also called the superordinate): a tulip is a kind of flower. Words at the same level under one hypernym are co-hyponyms. In a taxonomy they are incompatible with each other (tulip, rose, daisy: one flower can't be two of them), though not all co-hyponyms are: queen and mother are both hyponyms of woman, and one woman can be both.

Tests: X is a kind/type of Y; one-way entailment: I picked a tulip entails I picked a flower, not the other way round.

Hyponymy is transitive: a robin is a bird, a bird is an animal, so a robin is an animal. Stacked levels form a taxonomy: animal > bird > robin. Verbs have hyponyms too: murder < kill, stroll < walk, whisper < speak.

A language can lack a hypernym where you expect one: English has no everyday word covering both uncle and aunt (a lexical gap).

Meronymypart–whole: finger → hand+

The meronym names a part, the holonym the whole: finger is a meronym of hand, petal of flower, chapter of book.

Test: X is part of Y, A Y has X. Not X is a kind of Y: a petal isn't a kind of flower.

Not always transitive. A cylinder is part of an engine and an engine is part of a car, so a cylinder is part of a car. But a handle is part of a door and a door is part of a house, and ?The house has a handle is odd. Different kinds of part (functional parts, segments, members of a group, the stuff something is made of) don't chain freely.

Homonymy vs polysemytwo words, or one word with several senses?+

Homonymy: unrelated words that happen to share a form. Polysemy: one word with several related senses.

Same sound?Same spelling?Examples

Full homonymsyesyesbat (animal / club), bark (dog / tree), bear (animal / carry)

Polysemy: mouth (of a person, of a river), foot (of a person, of a mountain), bright (light, student). The senses are linked, usually by metaphor or metonymy.

Criteria: (1) etymology: did the senses come from one source? (2) speaker intuition: do speakers feel the senses are connected? The two can disagree. ear (organ) and ear (of corn) come from two different Old English words, yet many speakers feel a link. pupil (student) and pupil (of the eye) go back to the same Latin source, yet few speakers feel any link. Because of cases like these, exam questions use clear examples.

Dictionaries give homonyms separate entries (bat¹, bat²) and list polysemous senses as numbered senses inside one entry.

Componential analysis

Break word meanings into binary semantic features (components), written in capitals with + or −. Words that differ in one feature contrast minimally; shared features explain why words form a field.

± = unspecified. −HUMAN alone doesn't get you to cattle; you need something like BOVINE to keep bull apart from stallion.

Features explain relations neatly: man and woman are incompatible because they differ in MALE; boy entails child because it has every feature of child. Redundancy rules save writing: +HUMAN implies +ANIMATE.

Problems

  • Which features are primitive? There is no agreed list, and features like BOVINE look like the word itself in capitals (circularity).
  • Binarity is forced. ADULT is a matter of degree (teenager), and many meanings aren't +/− at all.
  • Most of the vocabulary resists it. Kinship and animal terms work; game, chair, justice or colour terms don't decompose into a neat checklist.
  • Generic uses break the grid. a herd of cows can include bulls; man was long used for humans in general.
  • No room for typicality. Every feature is necessary and sufficient, so a penguin is as much a bird as a robin. Prototype theory starts here.

Prototype theory

Rosch's experiments in the 1970s showed that categories have a centre and a periphery. Asked to rate how good an example of bird something is, speakers rank robin and sparrow high and penguin, ostrich or emu low, and they recognise central members faster. The best example is the prototype. A penguin is fully a bird, just not a typical one: typicality is not membership.

  • Fuzzy boundaries: members share a cluster of typical properties (flies, small, sings, builds nests) rather than one checklist. Wittgenstein's game has no single property common to all games, only overlapping family resemblances.
  • Labov's cups (1973): speakers labelled drawings of containers whose width-to-height ratio varied. Wider ones slid from cup to bowl, taller ones to vase, with no sharp line, and context moved the line: filled with mashed potatoes, the same object became a bowl more often; holding flowers, a vase.
  • Basic level: in a taxonomy one level is psychologically privileged. dog (not animal or terrier), chair (not furniture or kitchen chair): learned first, short, used by default, easy to picture.

Semantic fields

Words in one area of meaning (kinship, colour, cooking, temperature) form a semantic field and divide it up between them, so each word's value depends on its neighbours. Languages cut the same field differently:

German one word, English two

Uhr = clock / watch; Himmel = sky / heaven; Fleisch = meat / flesh.

English one word, German two

wall = Wand (inside) / Mauer (free-standing); know = wissen / kennen.

Lexical gaps

A slot in the field with no single word: English has no everyday word for aunt or uncle; sibling exists but is technical, where German Geschwister is everyday.

Metaphor and metonymy

Conceptual metaphor

Lakoff & Johnson (1980): we understand one domain (the target, usually abstract) in terms of another (the source, usually concrete). The mapping is systematic, not one-off, and it crosses two domains. (Traditionally metaphor is said to rest on similarity; Lakoff & Johnson ground it in correlations in experience instead: adding more makes the pile go up, hence MORE IS UP.)

ARGUMENT IS WAR: attack a position, indefensible claims, shoot down an argument, win a debate.
TIME IS MONEY: spend, waste, save, invest time; running out of time.
LOVE IS A JOURNEY: at a crossroads, going nowhere, a dead end.
MORE IS UP / HAPPY IS UP: prices soar; I'm feeling down.

Metonymy

One thing stands for another it is closely associated with inside the same domain (contiguity, not similarity).

The kettle is boiling (container for contents).
Downing Street said (place for institution).
The ham sandwich wants the bill (food ordered for the customer).
I'm reading Shakespeare (producer for product).
The buses are on strike (object for user).
Watergate (place for event).

Synecdoche

Part for whole: all hands on deck, fifty head of cattle, new faces, nice wheels. This page treats synecdoche as a kind of metonymy, as Lakoff & Johnson do (their PART FOR WHOLE). Rhetoric books list it separately and also count whole-for-part and kind-for-member uses.

Quick test. Ask "is it like it?" vs "is it next to / part of / connected with it?" A lawyer who is a shark resembles a shark: metaphor. The Crown doesn't resemble the monarch; it belongs to her or him: metonymy. A simile makes the comparison explicit with like or as: He fights like a lion.

Exam traps: word meaning

  • Hyponymy vs meronymy. "Kind of" vs "part of". A petal is part of a flower, not a kind of flower; a tulip is a kind of flower, not part of one.
  • Converse is not complementary. buy/sell don't split a domain into two halves; they describe one transaction from two sides. Swap the participants to test.
  • Gradable vs complementary. Try very, a comparative and neither X nor Y. very hot and neither hot nor cold are fine; ?very dead and ?neither dead nor alive are jokes or contradictions.
  • Homonymy is about origin and relatedness, not spelling. knight/night (different spelling) are homonyms in the broad sense; mouth of a river (same spelling) is polysemy.
  • Sense ≠ reference. Same referent does not mean same sense (Morning Star/Evening Star), and same sense does not mean same referent (I said by two people).
  • Frege's Bedeutung = reference. Not "meaning".
  • True synonymy is rare. Never claim two words are "the same" without naming the dimension they differ on: dialect, register, connotation, collocation.
  • Metaphor = similarity across domains; metonymy = association within a domain. The kettle is boiling is metonymy, not a metaphor.
  • A prototype is the best example, not a definition. Atypical members (penguin) are still members.
  • False friends. eventually = in the end, never "maybe"; actual = real, never "current"; become = werden.

Truth conditions and compositionality

To know what a declarative sentence means is to know what the world would have to be like for it to be true: its truth conditions. You can know the truth conditions of There are seventeen people in the next room without knowing whether it is true. The abstract meaning shared by Ann loves Tom and Tom is loved by Ann is a proposition.

Compositionality (Frege's principle): the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings of its parts and the way they are put together. That is why you understand sentences you've never heard, and why The dog bit the man and The man bit the dog differ: same words, different structure.

Idioms are the main exception: the meaning of kick the bucket, spill the beans or pull someone's leg can't be worked out from the parts. They are stored whole, like words. Idioms differ in how frozen they are: the beans were spilled still works, ?the bucket was kicked loses the idiomatic reading.

Entailment and presupposition

Entailment: A entails B if, whenever A is true, B must be true. The anarchist assassinated the president entails The president died. Test: A, but not B is a contradiction (?The anarchist assassinated the president but the president didn't die).

  • One-way: Lucy owns a dog → Lucy owns an animal, not back. Hyponymy produces one-way entailments.
  • Mutual (two-way) entailment = paraphrase, the sentence-level version of synonymy: John sold the car to Mary ⇔ Mary bought the car from John.

Presupposition: background information a sentence takes for granted. John stopped smoking presupposes John used to smoke.

The negation testEntailmentPresupposition
ATom broke the vase. → The vase broke.Tom regrets breaking the vase. → Tom broke the vase.
not-ATom didn't break the vase. → nothing about the vase.Tom doesn't regret breaking the vase. → still Tom broke the vase.
ResultLost under negation.Survives negation (also questions: Does Tom regret...?).

Triggers: definite descriptions (the king of France), possessives (Mary's brother), factive verbs (know, regret, realise), change-of-state verbs (stop, start, continue), iteratives (again), clefts (It was Tom who...), implicatives (manage presupposes trying), temporal clauses (before, after, when).

Overlap. The positive sentence usually entails its presuppositions too: Mary's brother is a pilot both entails and presupposes that Mary has a brother. What makes it a presupposition is that it survives negation. Presuppositions can also be cancelled (The king of France isn't bald: there is no king of France) and they behave differently in context; that side belongs to Pragmatics.

Analytic, synthetic, contradiction

Analytic

True by virtue of meaning alone. Bachelors are unmarried. A puppy is a young dog. A tautology is the extreme case: Either it's raining or it isn't. (Pragmatically, tautologies like Boys will be boys still communicate something.)

Synthetic

True or false depending on the facts. My neighbour is unmarried. It's raining in Graz. You have to check the world.

Contradiction

False by virtue of meaning alone: My brother is an only child. This square has five sides. Two sentences contradict each other when the truth of one guarantees the falsity of the other: Lucy is alive / Lucy is dead.

Anomaly and selectional restrictions

Chomsky's Colourless green ideas sleep furiously is grammatically perfect and semantically anomalous: colourless clashes with green, ideas can't be green or sleep, and nobody sleeps furiously. The clash comes from selectional restrictions, the semantic requirements a word places on its neighbours:

  • drink wants a liquid object: ?drink the sandwich.
  • frighten wants an animate object: ?The noise frightened the table.
  • elapse wants a period of time as subject: ?The table elapsed.
  • pregnant wants a female subject (in the literal sense).

Metaphor and poetry break selectional restrictions on purpose: time flies, a furious silence.

Ambiguity and vagueness

Lexical ambiguity

One word has two meanings (homonymy or polysemy); the structure is the same. She went to the bank. She can't bear children.

Structural ambiguity

The words are the same, but they can be grouped in two ways.
old [men and women] / [old men] and women
I saw [the man with the telescope] / I [saw the man] [with the telescope]
Visiting relatives can be boring (going to visit them / relatives who visit)

Scope ambiguity

Every student read a book. (a) for each student, some book or other (every > a); (b) one particular book that every student read (a > every). Reading (b) entails (a). Same for negation: All the students didn't pass (none passed / not all passed).

Vague is not ambiguous. A vague expression has one meaning that is unspecific: Ann's cousin arrived doesn't say whether the cousin is male or female, but cousin doesn't have two senses. tall, many, soon are vague about where the line is.

Identity test (with so did / do so): an ambiguous word must be read the same way in both halves. Ann went to the bank and so did Ben can't mean one went to a riverbank and the other to a savings bank. A vague word can be resolved differently: Ann's cousin arrived and so did Ben's is fine with a female cousin and a male one. So bank is ambiguous, cousin vague.

Situation types (Aktionsart)

Vendler (1957) sorted predicates into four classes. They are usually described with three properties (the feature grid comes from later work such as Smith 1991): dynamic (something happens), durative (it takes time) and telic (it has a built-in endpoint).

TypeDynamicDurativeTelicExamples
State−+−know the answer, love, own, be tall, resemble
Activity++−run, swim, push a cart, laugh, drive around
Accomplishment+++run a mile, draw a circle, build a house, eat an apple
Achievement+−+recognise, spot, reach the top, win the race, die

Tests

  • Progressive: odd with most states (*She is knowing the answer); fine with activities and accomplishments; with achievements it describes the run-up, not the event (He is dying, They are reaching the summit).
  • for an hour: fine with states and activities (swam for an hour); odd with accomplishments (?built a house for an hour, unless unfinished); with achievements only as repetition or the result state (?recognised her for an hour).
  • in an hour: fine with accomplishments (drew a circle in a minute) and achievements, where it gives the time until the event (reached the top in an hour); odd with activities and states (?swam in an hour).
  • Imperfective paradox: was running entails ran (activity), but was drawing a circle doesn't entail drew a circle (accomplishment: you can be interrupted).
  • Dynamic test: What she did was... and imperatives work with the dynamic classes, not with states (?Know the answer!).

The whole predicate counts, not the verb. run is an activity, run a mile an accomplishment; eat apples (bare plural) is an activity, eat an apple an accomplishment; sing vs sing a song. Smith (1991) added a fifth class, semelfactives: instantaneous, atelic events like knock, cough, blink, which give repetition in the progressive (He was coughing).

Deixis

Words whose reference shifts with the speech situation (I, you, here, there, now, yesterday, this) have a fixed sense and context-dependent reference. They sit on the border with pragmatics; person, place, time, social and discourse deixis are covered on the Pragmatics page.

Exam traps: sentence meaning

  • Presupposition vs entailment. Negate A. If B still follows, it's a presupposition; if it disappears, it was only an entailment.
  • Implicature is not entailment. Some students passed suggests not all, but doesn't entail it: Some students passed, in fact all of them did is not a contradiction.
  • Non-factive verbs. believe, think, say, try don't commit the speaker to their complement; factive know, realise, regret do, even when negated. Implicative manage does only in the positive (didn't manage to = didn't).
  • Roles ≠ functions. The subject is not automatically the agent: The key opened the door (instrument), The vase broke (patient), Tom received a letter (recipient).
  • Vague ≠ ambiguous. Use the so did test before you call something ambiguous.
  • Lexical vs structural. If you can paraphrase the two readings by regrouping the same words (brackets, different trees), it's structural. If one word has two senses, it's lexical.
  • Classify the predicate, not the verb. write vs write a letter. And achievements in the progressive don't mean the event happened (He was dying but survived).
  • For German speakers: Standard German has no fully grammaticalised progressive (the colloquial am-progressive, Sie ist am Lesen, is restricted), so run the Vendler tests in English, not in translation.

Semiotics: the study of signs

Semantics studies meaning in language; semiotics (semiology, in Saussure's term) studies signs of every kind: words, gestures, traffic lights, pictures, smoke. The two founding traditions are Saussure's (linguistic, two-part sign) and Peirce's (philosophical, three-part sign).

Saussure's sign

The sign is a two-sided psychological unit: a signifier (a sound-image, the mental imprint of the sound) joined to a signified (a concept). Neither is the real tree; the referent in the world is not part of Saussure's sign.

Arbitrariness

Nothing in the concept TREE calls for the sounds /triː/: German Baum, French arbre. The link is fixed by convention. Saussure allowed relative motivation: nineteen is motivated by nine + -teen, while ten is fully arbitrary.

Linearity

The spoken signifier unfolds in time, one element after another, so language is a chain. (A picture shows everything at once.)

Langue vs parole

Langue: the shared, abstract system of a community. Parole: individual acts of speaking. Saussure made langue the object of linguistics. (Compare Chomsky's competence/performance, which is individual and mental.)

Synchrony vs diachrony

Synchronic: the system at one point in time. Diachronic: its change through time. Saussure put synchrony first: speakers don't need history to use their language.

Value (valeur)

A sign's value comes from its contrasts with the other signs in the system. French mouton covers both the animal and the meat; English divides the field into sheep and mutton, so sheep has a different value from mouton.

Syntagmatic vs paradigmatic relations

Thecatsaton the mat
Adogsleptunder the table
Myparrotwaitedby the door

Syntagmatic (horizontal, "and... and"): relations between units that combine in a chain, the + cat + sat. Collocation and selectional restrictions are syntagmatic. Paradigmatic (vertical, "or... or"): relations between units that can replace each other in one slot, cat / dog / parrot. Synonymy, antonymy and hyponymy are paradigmatic relations. (Saussure himself spoke of "associative" relations; "paradigmatic" is the later term.)

Ogden & Richards' semiotic triangle

Ogden and Richards (1923) put the referent back in. A word (symbol) evokes a concept (thought or reference), and the concept relates to a thing in the world (referent). The base line is dotted: there is no direct link between a word and a thing; the connection always runs through the mind. This is the arbitrariness of the sign seen from another angle.

Peirce: icon, index, symbol

For Peirce a sign has three parts: the sign vehicle (representamen), the object it stands for, and the interpretant, the understanding it produces. His best-known division sorts signs by how they connect to their object:

TypeLink to the objectExamples
IconResemblance: the sign looks, sounds or is structured like what it stands for.a portrait, a statue, a map, a floor plan, a road sign showing a deer, a scale model, a caricature, a mime of drinking; onomatopoeia like cuckoo (partly)
IndexPhysical or causal connection (contiguity): the sign is caused by, or points to, its object.smoke for fire, footprints, a fever for an infection, a weathervane for wind direction, a pointing finger, a knock at the door, dark clouds for rain, a regional accent for where someone grew up
SymbolConvention: the link is learned and arbitrary.most words (tree, Baum), traffic lights, flags, a wedding ring, numerals, the € sign, a white flag for surrender, nodding for yes

Mixed signs are the norm. A photograph resembles its object (icon) and was physically caused by light from it (index). A dove is iconic of a dove but symbolic of peace. Peirce himself treated words like this, here and I as indexical. For exam answers, name the dominant link and say what else is mixed in.

Motivation vs arbitrariness

Most linguistic signs are arbitrary, but language has pockets of motivation (iconicity), where form reflects meaning:

  • Onomatopoeia imitates sounds, but only partly: the cock crows cock-a-doodle-doo in English, kikeriki in German, cocorico in French; the dog says woof or bow-wow vs wau wau. Each language fits the imitation to its own sound system, so onomatopoeia is iconic and conventional at once.
  • Sound symbolism / phonaesthemes: recurring sound–meaning links without being morphemes. gl- and light: glow, gleam, glitter, glisten; sn- and the nose: sniff, snore, sneeze, snout.
  • Iconicity in syntax: the order of clauses mirrors the order of events. She got married and had a baby is understood differently from She had a baby and got married; veni, vidi, vici.
  • Relative motivation (Saussure): complex words are partly transparent (nineteen, teacher, pear tree) even though their parts are arbitrary.

Hockett's design features

Hockett (1960) listed the properties of human language to compare it with animal communication. The core 13 are below; he later added prevarication, reflexiveness and learnability.

FeatureWhat it means
Vocal-auditory channelProduced by the vocal tract, received by the ear. (Sign languages show it isn't essential to language.)
Broadcast transmission, directional receptionSound goes out in all directions; the hearer can tell where it comes from.
Rapid fadingSpoken signals vanish at once (unlike writing or animal scent marks).
InterchangeabilityAny speaker can also be a receiver and can say what they understand.
Total feedbackSpeakers hear their own messages and can monitor them.
SpecialisationThe signal serves only to communicate; it does no other physical work.
SemanticitySignals are linked to meanings.
ArbitrarinessNo natural link between form and meaning.
DiscretenessMessages are built from distinct units (/p/ vs /b/), not a continuous scale.
DisplacementWe can talk about things remote in time and space, past, future, imaginary. Bee dances show a limited form.
Productivity (openness)New messages can be made and understood without limit.
Cultural (traditional) transmissionLanguage is learned from the community, not inherited genetically.
Duality of patterningMeaningless units (sounds) combine into meaningful units (words): /k/ /æ/ /t/ give cat, act, tack.
Prevarication (added)Language can be used to lie or talk about the fictional.
Reflexiveness (added)Language can talk about language.
Learnability (added)A speaker of one language can learn another.

Introductory courses usually stress displacement, arbitrariness, productivity, cultural transmission and duality as the features that set human language apart. Animal systems have some features (vervet monkey alarm calls are semantic and arbitrary) but not the whole set.

Exam traps: signs

  • The signified is a concept, not a thing. Saussure's sign is entirely mental. The thing in the world is the referent, which belongs to the Ogden & Richards triangle, not to Saussure's sign.
  • Arbitrary ≠ random or freely chosen. The link is conventional; one speaker can't decide to call a tree a blick.
  • Peirce's "symbol" is a technical term. A dove for peace is a symbol because the link is conventional, not because it is "symbolic" in the everyday sense.
  • Index = connection, not just pointing. Smoke and fever are indices because they are caused by their objects.
  • Onomatopoeia is only partly iconic. Compare languages before you call it "natural".
  • Syntagmatic = combination (chain); paradigmatic = choice (substitution). Don't swap the axes.
  • Duality ≠ discreteness. Discreteness is about distinct units; duality is about two levels, meaningless sounds and meaningful words.
